# Russia Attacks the Baltic: Hour 61 and the First Nuke in Europe Since 1945

Episode 009. Draft 2, 2026-11-15. A simulation of the first sixty-one hours of a Russian attack on the Baltic states, built from published wargames, NATO force data and arms-control estimates. It has not happened. Each paragraph is one narration unit; bracketed refs are for the fact screener and are not read aloud.

## Cold open [SIMULATION]

Three forty-three in the morning, Lithuania: the first nuclear weapon used in war since Nagasaki. [C]

This is a simulation built from published numbers, and it has not happened. [A1]

Three minutes earlier, a radar in the Baltic picks up a ballistic missile climbing out of Kaliningrad. It is one missile, aimed at one air base. Nobody on the ground can tell whether the warhead is conventional or nuclear. [A13, A18, C]

It is the sixty-first hour of the war. Why would Moscow fire it, and what does the world do in the hour after? [C]

## Chapter 01: a night of sirens [REAL DATA]

On the eleventh of November, twenty twenty-six, drone alerts went out across Lithuania, Latvia and Estonia, urging people to shelter. [A3]

NATO air defence jets were activated. Lithuania's warning covered Kaunas, its second-largest city. The alerts were withdrawn the same day. No injuries were reported. [A3]

The drones were strays from the war in Ukraine, pushed off course, Kyiv said, by Russian jamming. Moscow blamed Kyiv. It was not an attack. But it showed how thin the line is. [A3]

That is the line our simulation crosses at hour three. And by hour sixty-one, the question is why one missile gets fired at all. [C]

Six weeks earlier, Russia sent NATO states a diplomatic note. It said Russia was ready to use its entire arsenal, including nuclear weapons, if NATO tried to cut off Kaliningrad. NATO's secretary-general answered: stop the nuclear threats. [A2]

Kaliningrad is Russian land on the Baltic coast, about a million people, wedged between Poland and Lithuania. It holds the Baltic Fleet and nuclear-capable Iskander missiles. [A2, A11]

Between Kaliningrad and Belarus runs the Suwalki Gap, about sixty-five kilometres of land. It is the shortest land route between Poland and the Baltic states, and one single-track railway connects Poland and Lithuania. [A9]

How fast could a crisis there turn into a war? [A20]

## Chapter 02: how close it has come [REAL HISTORY]

In February twenty fourteen, soldiers with no insignia took over Crimea within days. Russia denied they were its troops. Putin later admitted it. A deniable first move is the model for our hour zero. [B1]

In ten days of September twenty twenty-five, about twenty Russian drones entered Poland, Russia and Belarus held the Zapad exercise, and three Russian fighter jets flew into Estonian airspace. Poland and Estonia both asked NATO for consultations. Our simulation begins where those incidents stop. [B8, A5, A6]

In January nineteen ninety-five, Russian radar picked up a Norwegian research rocket. By one account, its path resembled a US submarine missile, the nuclear briefcase was activated, and observers decided within minutes that it was no threat. A defender who sees a missile has only minutes to decide what it carries, and that is the problem at hour sixty-one. [B4]

All three share one pattern: a small first move, then a short window when each side decides what the other meant. So what does the first day of our war look like? [C]

## Chapter 03: the trigger [SIMULATION]

In our simulation, hour zero is a deniable move at the Russian border: unmarked troops, a pretext about protecting Russian speakers, jamming, and a claim from Moscow that NATO is sealing off Kaliningrad. The Kremlin denies everything. [C, B1, A2]

Nothing here is a prediction. One Danish intelligence assessment calls a full invasion of a NATO country highly unlikely in the near term. These are choices, made so we can follow the escalation. [A23, C]

At hour three, two Iskander missiles leave the Kaliningrad area. One hits Rukla, where the German-led NATO battlegroup is based. The other hits Siauliai Air Base. Flight time: about three minutes. Conventional warheads. [A13, A20, C]

Why those two? Because Rukla is the allied army, and Siauliai is an air base that NATO jets fly from. The target is NATO's ability to answer. The launch sites in Kaliningrad are our choice, and no real garrison is claimed. [A20, C]

Will NATO treat this as an armed attack? Under Article five, an attack on one is an attack on all. [A8]

## Chapter 04: hour by hour [SIMULATION]

Hour six. A Kalibr cruise missile, launched from a ship in the Baltic, flies about thirty-nine minutes toward Amari Air Base in Estonia. Unlike the Iskanders, this one gives the defenders time to watch it come. [C]

Hour eleven. Another Iskander, from the Pskov region, hits the army base at Tapa, Estonia. By about hour twelve, in this simulation, NATO has invoked Article five. [C, A8]

Hours twelve to forty-eight. Russian and Belarusian forces close on the Suwalki Gap from both sides. Reinforcements from Germany and the Netherlands are still arriving, and the single rail line is the bottleneck. [A9, A22, C]

Hour thirty-one. An Iskander hits the Orzysz training area in Poland, where a NATO exercise, Gallant Boar, was held in June twenty twenty-six, according to one report. [A29, C]

One summary of RAND's twenty sixteen wargames put Russian forces at the outskirts of Tallinn or Riga in at most sixty hours. That was against a weaker NATO posture, and it is not a prediction. [A24]

In our simulation, the ground fight stalls. NATO aircraft arrive, and the German-Netherlands corps moves up. Moscow decides the war is turning against it. Some analysts argue that Russian doctrine allows one limited nuclear use to force a settlement. Others say no such policy exists. Our simulation uses that reading as a choice. [A18, A17, C]

Hour sixty-one. One Iskander-M, with a nuclear warhead, is aimed at Siauliai Air Base. The aim point is an air base, not a city. The flight is about three minutes, with no warning beyond sirens and jets. Defenders would see a ballistic launch and could not know what it carries. [C, A13]

We found no published yield for an Iskander warhead, so in this simulation we use ten kilotons, about half of Nagasaki. What does ten kilotons do to one airfield? [C, B9]

## Chapter 05: one warhead, ring by ring [SIMULATION]

The warhead explodes in the air above the base at three forty-three in the morning. Every number in this chapter comes from our engine, and every one is an estimate. The airfield is what it was built to protect: a runway and hangars. [C]

The fireball is about one hundred and fifty metres in radius, so about three hundred metres across. Inside it, runways, hangars and aircraft are gone. [C]

At three hundred metres, the pressure is twenty pounds per square inch. Reinforced concrete fails. [C]

At one point four six kilometres, five pounds per square inch. Most buildings on land are flattened. The ring covers about six point seven square kilometres. [C]

Out to one point six kilometres, the flash is enough for third-degree burns on bare skin. [C]

At three point nine five kilometres, one pound per square inch. Windows shatter. [C]

The centre of Siauliai is about seven kilometres from the base, outside the last ring, but the nearest streets and suburbs are inside it. The burst is high, so the engine finds little local fallout. [C]

For the base itself, that is the whole story, in about a second. For everyone else, the story begins after it. [C]

## Chapter 06: the day after [SIMULATION]

First, the airfield. Why did Moscow fire it? In this simulation, to stop the fight, aimed at a base, not a city. At first light, the airfield is a dark gap on the map, and the base's jets go quiet. [C]

Second, closed skies. Flights over the Baltic region stop, as Finland briefly restricted airspace and shipping in the eastern Gulf of Finland during the real drone alerts of November twenty twenty-six. [A3, C]

Third, the dust. We choose a light westerly wind, so a thin drift of radioactive material lofted by the burst drifts east over Lithuanian farmland. Any plume on screen is illustrative. The engine gives no distance and no dose. [C]

Fourth, a table in Brussels. NATO's treaty says allies consult whenever any member is threatened, and Article five allows armed force. Three options are on it: reply in kind, reply conventionally, or stop and talk. We do not pick one. [A8, C]

Fifth, the bottleneck. Reinforcements still have to cross the Suwalki Gap, with one single-track rail line between Poland and Lithuania. Nothing about the strike widens it. Rail Baltica, the standard-gauge link, is still under construction. [A9]

Sixth, thinner help from across the Atlantic. In May twenty twenty-six the Pentagon announced it would withdraw up to five thousand troops from Germany, leaving about eighty thousand American troops in Europe. Allies would be asking what that means after the strike. [A21]

Seventh, a silent treaty. New START expired in February twenty twenty-six, so no inspector counts either side's strategic warheads, and tactical weapons were never covered at all. Estimates of Russia's tactical stock run from roughly fifteen hundred to two thousand warheads. [A19, A15]

And behind all of it, the model that asks where this leads. In the Princeton study, Plan A, a war that begins in Europe moves from tactical nuclear use to strategic strikes. In that model, the first tactical phase is a small fraction of the total. [A26, A27]

The airfield is quiet at dawn, and the decision is still open. [C]
