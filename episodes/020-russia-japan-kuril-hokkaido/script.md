# What If Russia and Japan Clash Over the Kuril Islands, and One Nuclear Weapon Is Used at Sea

Episode 020. Draft 2, 2026-11-27. Target 11 to 13 minutes at about 138 words per minute. Each paragraph is one narration unit; the `[TAG]` on a section header is the on-screen corner tag for everything under it until the next header. Source refs in brackets are for the fact screener and are not read aloud.

## Cold open [SIMULATION]

Eleven thirty-seven, off Hokkaido: the first nuclear weapon used in war since Nagasaki. [C]

This is a simulation built from published numbers, and it has not happened. [C]

Twenty-seven minutes earlier. A Russian submarine, deep in the Sea of Okhotsk, fires a cruise missile toward a group of Japanese and American warships in the Pacific. [C]

Radar picks it up. The ships have twenty-seven minutes to do something about it. [C]

Why does Russia fire at ships, and not at a city? And what do Tokyo and Washington do with the half hour they have? The answer starts with a few islands you can see from Japan. [C]

## Chapter 01: islands within sight [REAL DATA]

On the thirteenth of August, twenty twenty-six, Vladimir Putin landed on Iturup, the largest of the Kuril Islands. No Russian president had ever stood there. Japan's prime minister called it absolutely unacceptable. [A2]

In the same month, the Pacific Fleet fired missiles from the islands, and from ships and a submarine nearby, at what it called hypothetical enemy ships. [A3]

The coastal battery that fired is called Bastion. Bastions have stood on two of the southern islands since twenty sixteen, and their missiles reach hundreds of kilometres. In our simulation, one fires the first shot of the war. [A3, A15, C]

The southern Kurils lie a few kilometres to a few dozen kilometres from Hokkaido. Kunashir is about sixteen kilometres from one Japanese peninsula. The nearest islet is only about four kilometres from the cape at the tip of Hokkaido. [A11]

Behind them is the Sea of Okhotsk, where Russia shelters its missile submarines. The straits through the Kurils, and the Soya and Tsugaru straits beside Hokkaido, are its doors. Whoever holds the islands holds a piece of that door. [A13, A17]

Japan has been building an answer. In March twenty twenty-six, it began deploying an upgraded missile with a range of about one thousand kilometres. Long-range missiles are planned for Hokkaido itself. Its defence budget this year is a record, above nine trillion yen. [A23, A24]

Russia answers with something older. One estimate says its navy alone holds about seven hundred and sixty-seven non-strategic nuclear warheads, for cruise missiles and torpedoes. All of them are declared to be in central storage, not on the islands. [A20]

Has either side come close to firing before? [B3, B5]

## Chapter 02: the treaty that never came [REAL HISTORY]

In August nineteen forty-five, Japan announced its surrender on the fifteenth. Within days, Soviet troops began landing on the Kurils, and the last islets fell in early September, after the war was over. About seventeen thousand Japanese islanders were later sent away. [A12, B3]

In nineteen fifty-six, Moscow and Tokyo ended the state of war, and the Soviet Union agreed to hand over two of the four islands once a peace treaty was signed. No treaty ever followed. In March twenty twenty-two, Russia suspended the talks after Japan joined the sanctions over Ukraine. So when a crisis starts in our simulation, nobody has a channel open. [B5, B9, A5, C]

On the sixth of September, nineteen seventy-six, a Soviet pilot flew a fighter from near Vladivostok and landed it at Hakodate, on Hokkaido. Radar saw him, but the two fighters sent up never found him. It was a defection, not an attack. It is a reminder that this coast sits close to Soviet air bases. [B6]

Since then, no clash, no blockade, no shot fired at each other. The August firings were drills. [A1, A3]

So how does a quarrel over a few islands become a nuclear weapon? [C]

## Chapter 03: the spark [SIMULATION]

In our simulation, it starts on day one with a collision. A Japanese coast guard boat and a Russian border boat touch in the Nemuro Strait, between Hokkaido and Kunashir. The cause is left unsaid. [C]

Talks are frozen, so no one is on the phone. On day two, Russia declares a closure zone in the strait and issues live-fire warnings, as it did in August. Japan sends destroyers. Washington moves ships out of Yokosuka. [A3, A5, A28, C]

And Moscow says the islands are Russian soil: its constitution bars giving up territory. Under its doctrine, conventional aggression that is a critical threat to its territory is one of the conditions for nuclear use. [A5, A21]

That is where the simulation chooses to put the pressure. It does not say what Moscow would do. We found no published wargame of this clash, so every step from here is our choice. [A29, C]

## Chapter 04: day by day [SIMULATION]

Day three. A Bastion battery on Kunashir fires one Oniks missile at a Japanese destroyer in the strait. The target is fifty-four kilometres away. The missile takes under four minutes. Whether the ship survives, we do not say. Nothing about its crew is simulated. [A15, C]

Day four. Nothing is fired. Both governments say, in public, that they want no wider war. The calm is thin: the same two phones that could have rung are still silent. [A5, C]

Day five. Japan answers, without nuclear weapons. We assume a long-range missile has reached central Hokkaido, and it fires one at the Bastion battery. Two hundred and eighty-eight kilometres, about twenty minutes in the air. [A24, C]

Day six. In Moscow, the question is whether a strike on the islands is a critical threat to Russia. Russian doctrine was rewritten in twenty twenty-four, and the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists argues it lowered the bar. It is a reading, not a promise. [A21, C]

Day seven, minute zero. Eleven ten in the morning, local time. We assume a Kalibr cruise missile with a nuclear warhead leaves a submarine in the Sea of Okhotsk. Whether Kalibr carries a nuclear warhead in service is not public, so this is our choice. [C]

Its target is a Japanese and American escort group in the Pacific, off south-east Hokkaido. Three hundred and eighty-six kilometres away. [C]

We assume a subsonic cruise missile, so the ships get about twenty-seven minutes of warning. In that time, a destroyer can turn, the group can spread out, and radar operators can track the missile. Commanders can call Tokyo and Washington. What nobody can do is know what the warhead is. [C]

Whether the group can shoot the missile down, we do not say. The simulation fixes the outcome: it gets through. The last treaty limiting Russian and American nuclear forces expired in February twenty twenty-six, so no treaty cap applies. [A22, C]

Minute twenty-seven. What does a ten-kiloton weapon do to open water? [C]

## Chapter 05: one warhead, ring by ring [SIMULATION]

The missile detonates in the air, above the sea. We assume ten kilotons, the low end of one published estimate for Russian battlefield warheads, and about two thirds of the Hiroshima bomb. [C]

The fireball is about three hundred metres across, a small bright point on a very large sea. We give no yield as Russian policy; ten is our assumption. Within about three hundred metres of the burst, pressure would reach twenty pounds per square inch. [C]

Out to about one and a half kilometres, the blast would flatten most buildings on land. That ring covers about seven square kilometres. That is about the area of a small town. [C]

The flash alone would cause third-degree burns out to about one point six kilometres. That is a little farther than the blast ring. The light arrives first, then the shock wave, a few seconds behind it. Anyone who saw the flash would have almost no time to look away. [C]

Windows would shatter on land out to about four kilometres. That is the outer ring we draw. We found no published data on blast damage to warships, so these rings are written in land terms. [C]

Apart from the ships, every ring falls on open water. The burst is about eighty-five kilometres from Cape Nosappu, by our own measure, so no Japanese land is inside any ring. An air burst over water lifts little material, and the engine reports little local fallout. [C]

## Chapter 06: the day after [SIMULATION]

In our simulation, the question from the first minute has an answer: why a fleet, not a city? We treat the shot as a signal, our reading and our choice. It says: we can. A city would say something else, and we found no published count for one, so we give none. [C]

The first visible change is the plume. We assume wind from the west, so what little rises drifts east over the open Pacific, away from Hokkaido and the Kurils. [C]

The second is the sea itself. We assume shipping and air routes around Hokkaido close. The Soya Strait, about forty-two kilometres wide, is where Russian ships from Vladivostok reach the Okhotsk Sea. On the map, the strait goes dark. [A13, C]

The third is the gas. Sakhalin-2 supplies about a tenth of Japan's liquefied gas. Two Japanese trading houses hold stakes in the project. Tokyo reviews the supply. [A27, C]

The fourth is a debate. Japan has held to three non-nuclear principles since nineteen sixty-seven: no possessing, no making, no introducing. Its government is already rewriting its security documents. The principles are reopened in public. [A26, C]

And the last is a decision. Tokyo and Washington choose between a conventional reply, a statement that America's nuclear umbrella covers Japan, or a call through a third country. Published analysis of Russian nuclear use in Ukraine leans conventional. Our simulation shows all three options, and stops there. [C]

The strait is empty. The rings fade into the Pacific. None of this has happened. The islands, and the missing treaty, are real. [A1, B5]
