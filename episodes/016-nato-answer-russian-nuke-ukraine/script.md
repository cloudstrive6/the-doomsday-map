# NATO's Answer to a Russian Nuclear Strike on Ukraine: The First 72 Hours (Simulation)

Episode 016. Draft 1, 2026-11-23. Target about 12 minutes at 138 words per minute. Each paragraph is one narration unit; the `[TAG]` on a section header is the on-screen corner tag for everything under it. Source refs at paragraph ends are for the fact screener and are not read aloud.

## Cold open [SIMULATION]

One forty-three in the afternoon, central Ukraine: a nuclear weapon explodes over open farmland. [C]

This is a simulation built from published numbers, and it has not happened. [C]

Three minutes earlier, a missile launcher in Russia's Kursk region raises its rail. One Iskander-M. Two hundred and thirteen kilometres to fly. [C]

Nobody in Kyiv or Brussels has three minutes to decide anything. But in the hours after the flash, one question lands on every NATO table. What does the alliance do, and how long before it has to choose? [C]

## Chapter 01: a missile that already exists [REAL DATA]

In May twenty twenty-six, Russia and Belarus held a three-day nuclear exercise. Belarusian troops fired an Iskander missile. Russia's defence ministry said the focus was the preparation and use of nuclear forces under the threat of aggression. [A2]

The Iskander-M is the weapon analysts consider most likely for a battlefield nuclear strike. The Council on Foreign Relations calls it the likely vehicle in that case. Published tables give its nuclear warhead as up to a hundred kilotons, and the range as about three hundred and fifty kilometres. [A12]

Hiroshima was about fifteen kilotons. So a low-yield weapon is not a small one. Back to the missile in the first minute: three minutes is all the warning there is. [A13]

Russia is believed to hold roughly one thousand to two thousand of these non-strategic warheads. Only a fraction sit on ground-launched missiles. [A9, A10]

And in November twenty twenty-four, Putin signed a revised doctrine. It lists conventional aggression that creates a critical threat to Russia's sovereignty among the grounds for nuclear use. One arms control expert says it appears to lower the threshold. It does not say what Russia would do. [A5, A6]

So if one of these missiles were fired, what has the West ever said it would do about it? [A26]

## Chapter 02: the answer nobody published [REAL HISTORY]

In October nineteen sixty-two, Kennedy named a red line on live television: any missile launched from Cuba would be treated as a Soviet attack. Six days later, Khrushchev agreed to withdraw. The red line was clear, and a back channel made the retreat possible. Cuba had days to talk. A strike on Ukraine would give minutes. [B1]

In September twenty twenty-two, Washington's national security adviser said the consequences of Russian nuclear use would be catastrophic. Asked for specifics, he said they had been spelled out in private channels. President Biden's own wording that year was that any use of nuclear weapons in this conflict, on any scale, would be completely unacceptable. The public answer was left vague on purpose. [B3, A26]

In September twenty twenty-five, Poland asked NATO for consultation under Article four after Russian drones entered its airspace. Article four means talking. It triggers no automatic action. A missile would not wait for a meeting. [B9, A19]

That is the public line: a deliberate blank on what would follow. Ukraine is not a NATO member, so Article five does not cover it. [A18]

So when the flash comes, which of the alliance's options crosses a line? [A27]

## Chapter 03: the strike [SIMULATION]

In our simulation, Moscow decides that a Ukrainian advance is a critical threat to Russia's territory, the wording of its own doctrine. That is our choice, not a prediction. [A5, C]

We assume the target is a Ukrainian reserve staging area in open farmland, with no city and no nuclear plant near. It is the battlefield case the Council on Foreign Relations describes, not an attack on civilians, which that analysis judges most likely to draw a Western nuclear answer. The yield is twenty kilotons, our choice inside the published range. [C, A25]

The warhead is the hard part to hide. Analysts believe such warheads sit in central depots, apart from their launchers, so a strike needs preparation that an observer might notice. In our simulation, it still arrives with no public warning. [A11, C]

The yardstick helps here. Twenty kilotons is a third more than Hiroshima, over farmland instead of a city. [A13, C]

Minute zero. The missile leaves the launcher. Minute three point three. The warhead bursts in the air above the field. [C]

## Chapter 04: the first 72 hours [SIMULATION]

Hour zero. Allied sensors register the flash, and every defence ministry in Europe is called at once. Within two hours, we assume, Allied capitals and NATO's Secretary-General call it unacceptable, echoing the twenty twenty-two wording. A call opens between American and Russian defence officials, as they did in October twenty twenty-two. [C, A26, B5]

Hour six. The Allies do not act as one body yet. Each capital has its own intelligence and its own election to think about. In our simulation, an ally asks for an emergency meeting of the North Atlantic Council under Article four, not Article five. The Group of Seven and the UN Security Council meet. Radiation monitoring and aid for fallout response begin. [C, A19, A25]

Hour twenty. The Nuclear Planning Group meets. NATO's own line is that the fundamental purpose of its nuclear forces is deterrence, and in our simulation it confirms that its posture does not change. [C, A20]

Hour thirty. The alliance says any further Russian nuclear use would meet a response, and does not say what kind. That is the ambiguity one Atlantic Council analyst urged in twenty twenty-two. Air defence and air policing move east. No NATO troops enter Ukraine. [C, A23, A25]

Hour thirty-six. The real decision begins. [C]

Four options sit on the map, all drawn from published analyses. [C, A23, A24, A25]

One: answer with a nuclear weapon. One analyst calls it politically infeasible on the soil of a non-nuclear state. In our simulation, NATO declines. [A24, C]

Two: strike the launcher, the Iskander unit, or the Black Sea Fleet with conventional weapons. One analyst proposes it. Another warns of Russian retaliation. In our simulation it would be the first step that makes NATO a direct party to the war, and Russian embassies said in twenty twenty-six that military action against Russian territory would meet all means, nuclear included. [A23, A24, A3, C]

Three: stay the course, with more weapons, sanctions and isolation. Four: press Ukraine to settle, which the same analyst judges unwise. [A24]

In our telling, each option answers a different fear. The first fears weakness. The second fears a second strike. The third fears a wider war, and the fourth fears a lost country. [C]

By hour seventy-two, we assume NATO picks the third. The conventional package stays prepared and unlaunched, so Moscow cannot know whether it will be used. Nothing is announced as a deadline, because no real one exists. [C, A23]

## Chapter 05: one warhead, ring by ring [SIMULATION]

Over the staging area, the engine puts the burst in the air, with a fireball about two hundred metres in radius. [C]

Out to three hundred and seventy metres, the blast reaches twenty pounds per square inch. Nothing built by hand stands. [C]

At one point eight kilometres, five pounds per square inch: most ordinary buildings badly damaged or collapsed. That ring covers about ten and a half square kilometres. [C]

Third-degree burns reach two point one kilometres, farther than the heavy damage. [C]

At five kilometres, one pound per square inch. Windows shatter. [C]

Inside all of these rings, our map has no village. The nearest mapped place is about six point six kilometres from ground zero. [C]

For an air burst the engine reports little local fallout. We chose a west-north-west wind, so any light fallout drifts east-south-east across farmland toward the front. The engine gives no dose, and no published study covers this strike, so we state no casualty figure. [C]

## Chapter 06: the day after [SIMULATION]

Back to the question from the first minute: did the answer hold? On day four, the map shows what did not happen. No ally has fired a nuclear weapon. No NATO soldier is in Ukraine. Russia has not fired a second. [C]

Radiation monitors in neighbouring countries would read background outside the immediate area, a modelling expectation for an air burst, not a measurement. [C]

Did the alliance stay united? We assume Poland and the Baltic states raise alert levels, and air corridors near the border close. Flights reroute. This is the Article four consultation of twenty twenty-five, scaled up. [C, B9]

Closed borders and quiet skies are the first visible change. The second is money. Did the shock reach everyone else? European gas, grain and shipping insurance prices move, in direction only, because we publish no price. We assume settlement links for Russian banks tighten, and that at this yield and height the grid stays up. [C]

And Moscow answers in words. Units in Belarus go on alert, and a statement says Western aid to Ukraine is part of a joint attack, the language of the twenty twenty-four doctrine. Nobody can tell from outside whether an Oreshnik or an Iskander carries a nuclear warhead. [C, A5, A15]

Our simulation draws no road to peace here. [C]

By day seven there is no ceasefire. The taboo is broken. NATO has not said what it would do next, and Moscow has not said it will not fire again. In twenty twenty-two, Atlantic Council experts argued that NATO should consult on its options in advance, before the day it needs them. [C, A27]
