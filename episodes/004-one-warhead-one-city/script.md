# One 800-Kiloton Warhead Over Chicago: Street by Street

Nine fifteen on a Tuesday morning. Somewhere in the Atlantic, a submarine fires one missile. [C]

It climbs. Eleven minutes from now, it will be over the Loop in Chicago. [C]

In eleven minutes, this is Chicago. [C]

This is a simulation built from published numbers, and it has not happened. [C]

Nine twenty. The missile is above the atmosphere. On the street, nobody knows. The trains run. The offices fill. A single warhead of eight hundred kilotons is on its way down. [C]

## Chapter 01: how big is one warhead [REAL DATA]

Start with the smallest unit of this story. One bomb.

On the sixth of August, nineteen forty-five, at about a quarter past eight in the morning, the United States dropped a uranium bomb on Hiroshima. It released about fifteen kilotons. Nearly every structure within a mile of ground zero was destroyed. Almost every building within three miles was damaged, and glass broke in suburbs twelve miles away. About seventy thousand people died at once, and well over a hundred thousand by the end of that year. [A9, A10, A11, B1]

Our warhead is eight hundred kilotons. About fifty-three times Hiroshima, by simple arithmetic. [A12]

No American warhead is that size. The American arsenal runs from about eight kilotons to about one point two megatons. Russia's heaviest warheads on a multiple-warhead missile are reported in the hundreds of kilotons. So eight hundred sits among the biggest modern strategic warheads. It is a simulation choice, and we say so on screen. [A6, A7]

And it is small by the standards of history. The United States tested a fifteen megaton device at Bikini Atoll in nineteen fifty-four. The Soviet Union exploded fifty megatons over Novaya Zemlya in nineteen sixty-one. Our warhead is about one nineteenth of the first, and one sixty-second of the second. [A12, A13, A14]

The world holds about twelve thousand warheads today. The United States and Russia hold more than eight in every ten. And since the fifth of February, twenty twenty-six, no treaty caps the strategic arsenals of either. [A1, A4]

## Chapter 02: how close it has come [REAL HISTORY]

No nuclear weapon has been used in war since Nagasaki. It has come close. [B2]

In January nineteen sixty-one, a B-52 broke apart over North Carolina and dropped two hydrogen bombs near Goldsboro. Each had a predicted yield of up to three point eight megatons. A later American memo said one simple switch stood between the country and a major catastrophe. Historians still debate how close the second bomb came. [B4]

In October nineteen sixty-two, during the Cuban Missile Crisis, a Soviet submarine, harassed by American ships, came close to using its nuclear torpedo. Officers on board helped talk the captain out of it. Accounts differ on how near it really was. [B6]

On the twenty-sixth of September, nineteen eighty-three, a Soviet satellite warned of one American missile, then four more. Duty officer Stanislav Petrov judged it a false alarm, reasoning that a real attack would be hundreds. A later inquiry found a malfunction. [B7]

In January nineteen ninety-five, a Norwegian science rocket was tracked by Russian radar. Russia's leaders were reportedly brought their nuclear briefcases. No order was given. [B9]

None of these was a deliberate attack. In two of them, officers had very little time to judge whether a warning was real. [B6, B7, B9]

## Chapter 03: the trigger [SIMULATION]

Our scenario has no war plan. No crisis, no ultimatum, no named country. That is deliberate. The question is only what one warhead does, and where it stops. [C]

The weapon is a single missile from an unattributed submarine, location simulated, in the Atlantic. It flies about twenty-seven hundred kilometres. The engine gives a flight of eleven minutes. [C]

The target is the Loop, the centre of Chicago, chosen for a flat, dense downtown on a lakeshore, not one of the cities in our earlier episodes. The time is a weekday morning, when the offices are full. All of it is our choice, not a forecast. [C]

The warhead is set to burst in the air above the city, not on the ground. That choice matters. An airburst spreads blast and heat wider. A ground burst digs a crater and throws up fallout. We chose the first, and say so. We give no burst height. [C]

## Chapter 04: minute by minute [SIMULATION]

Minute zero. Nine fifteen, Central time. In our simulation, the missile leaves the water. [C]

From here, the engine keeps one clock. Eleven point one minutes from launch to impact. Nothing in the flight can be changed once it starts. We do not model a defence, a warning or a second missile. [C]

Minute five. Nine twenty. Our simulated missile is above the atmosphere. Nobody in Chicago has been told anything. [C]

Minute eight. In our simulation, a radar track is a single point. One object. No second wave. Nothing else is coming. [C]

Minute nine. Still nothing on the street. A delivery truck unloads. A train pulls in. In this simulation, it is an ordinary Tuesday for about two more minutes. [C]

Minute ten. The morning rush is over and the offices are full. In the Loop, the trains run and the lights are on. There is nothing left to warn anyone with. [C]

Minute eleven. About nine twenty-six. The warhead fires above the city. [C]

## Chapter 05: one warhead, ring by ring [SIMULATION]

First, the flash. It comes before anything else, and it is over the whole lakeshore at once. Then the fireball forms. [C]

The fireball. Radius, eight hundred and eighty metres. Everything inside is vaporised. That is about two point four square kilometres, centred on the Loop. [C, engine]

Next, the twenty pounds per square inch ring. One point three kilometres. Heavily built concrete structures are severely damaged or destroyed. [C, engine]

Then the five pounds per square inch ring. Six point three kilometres. About a hundred and twenty-four square kilometres. Most ordinary buildings collapse or are heavily damaged. That is the area where, by this measure, ordinary buildings fail. [C, engine]

Heat travels faster than the blast. The third-degree burn ring reaches nine point eight kilometres. Wider than the five psi ring. Unshielded skin, with a clear line of sight to the flash, burns through. A firestorm is possible, as it was at Hiroshima, though we do not claim one. [C, engine, A11]

Last, the one psi ring. Seventeen kilometres. About nine hundred square kilometres. Windows shatter, and flying glass injures people who never saw the flash. The blast wave arrives after the light, at the speed of sound, so this ring hears it last. [C, engine]

Put the rings side by side. From the fireball to the last window, the radius grows nearly twenty times. The area grows by a factor of hundreds. That is why one warhead is not one building, or one street. It is a whole city centre, and then the districts around it. [C, engine]

Compare that with Hiroshima. The fifteen kiloton bomb wrecked nearly everything within a mile. Our fireball alone is nearly a kilometre in radius. Our five pounds per square inch ring reaches about four miles. The engine uses published blast scaling, not a guess. [C, engine, A11]

The eastern part of the larger rings lies over Lake Michigan. We do not count how many people stand inside any ring. No published study does it for this city and this yield. [C]

## Chapter 06: the day after [SIMULATION]

Heat and blast arrive within seconds to tens of seconds. The rest of the story takes days. [C]

For scale, a Princeton simulation, Plan A, modelled a full American and Russian exchange and estimated tens of millions of dead in its first hours. Researchers writing in Nature Food found that soot from thousands of warheads could leave billions short of food. That is the far end of the scale. One warhead is the smallest case. [A19, A20]

The wind, in our simulation, is from the west-northwest. An airburst throws up little local fallout, so the engine reports little. Whatever light fallout there is drifts out over the lake. A ground burst would be a different and far worse story. [C, A16]

The official advice is the same either way. Get inside. Stay inside. Stay tuned. Shelter for at least twenty-four hours, because radiation levels drop rapidly in the first day. Do not return to an evacuated area until officials say so. [A17]

Inside the heavy damage rings, buildings are down and lines are down. Power, phones and roads there would be expected to fail. That is what the rings imply, not a measured outcome. [C]

Federal planning for a nuclear detonation focuses on the first twenty-four to seventy-two hours. In our simulation, that is when outside help would have to reach the edge of the damage. [A17, C]

The hospitals are the hardest limit. The whole country has only about two thousand specialist burn beds. No study here models how many patients one warhead would bring, only that the gap is wide. [A18]

On the map, the rest of the country is untouched by this one weapon. By the second day, in our simulation, the Loop is rubble at the centre and standing buildings at the edge. Smoke goes east over the lake. The trains are not running. [C]

Nothing in this simulation is a prediction. The numbers are the engine's. [C]

It has not happened.
