## Chapter 01: the bomb that was tested [REAL DATA]

On the thirtieth of October, nineteen sixty-one, a Soviet bomber climbs to about ten and a half kilometres over Novaya Zemlya, an island in the Arctic. [A1, A4]

It drops a bomb twenty-seven tonnes heavy and eight metres long, hung under a parachute. The parachute slows the fall for about three minutes, so the bomber can get away. [A4]

At about four thousand metres above the ground, it explodes. The Soviet figure is fifty megatons. American estimates ran higher, and published numbers run up to about fifty-eight. [A1, A2]

The fireball is about eight kilometres wide. The cloud climbs about sixty-five kilometres. The shock wave pushes the bomber down by about a thousand metres, and the pressure wave is recorded circling the Earth three times. [A5, A6, A7, A10]

That fireball is the one at the start of this video: nine kilometres wide in our engine, against about eight in nineteen sixty-one. [A6, C]

The bomb was meant to be twice as big. In August nineteen sixty-one, Andrei Sakharov's team swapped the final stage's uranium for lead, which halved the yield and removed about ninety-seven percent of the fallout of the full design. [A3, B4]

It was too big for any missile of its time. The largest warheads on missiles today are far smaller: the Federation of American Scientists lists Russia's SS-18 warheads at five hundred to eight hundred kilotons. [A12, A22]

So why has nobody fired a bomb like this since? [A15]

## Chapter 02: how close, and how fast [REAL HISTORY]

In March nineteen fifty-four, the American test Castle Bravo at Bikini Atoll comes in at fifteen megatons, two and a half times what its designers expected. Fallout covers more than seven thousand square miles, and reaches islanders and the crew of a Japanese fishing boat. Tsar Bomba is about three times larger. [B2, A16]

In August nineteen sixty-three, the Soviet Union, Britain and the United States sign a treaty banning tests in the air, in space and underwater. Underground tests go on. So nobody has measured a bomb like this in the open air since, and the rings in this video are modelled, not measured. [B6, A15]

And now, the rules are thinner. Russia withdrew its ratification of the test-ban treaty in November twenty twenty-three. The last treaty capping American and Russian long-range warheads, New START, expired on the fifth of February, twenty twenty-six. No explosive test has been reported by either side. None of this points at New York. [B8, B10, A20, A23, A24]

How fast could it happen? In our simulation, the bomber needs sixty-two minutes from its start to the burst point. An eight-hundred-kiloton missile warhead, launched from the Arctic, would need twenty-three. [C]

So what happens in those sixty-two minutes? [C]

## Chapter 03: the release [SIMULATION]

In our simulation, there is no war and no named attacker. It is a scale study: one bomb, one point, no reason given, and no country accused. [C]

Ground zero is a public landmark point in lower Manhattan, forty point seven north, seventy-four west. [C]

Why lower Manhattan? It is one of the most recognisable shapes on any map, and the point is a place a viewer can see. It is not a claim that anyone plans this. [C]

The bomb is the Tsar Bomba class. It was never deployed, and the delivery is our choice: a single heavy bomber, releasing it over the North Atlantic and flying out to the burst point. [A12, C]

The yield is fifty megatons, the Soviet figure for the real test. For scale, the Nagasaki bomb was about twenty-one kilotons, and this is more than two thousand times more. By one estimate, it is about ten times all the conventional explosives used in the Second World War. [A1, A11, B1]

The real bomb burst at about four thousand metres. Our engine does not take a height. It picks the one that makes the widest blast ring, and so the figures here are the engine's, not a measured height. [A1, C]

So what does the clock show when the bomber arrives? [C]

## Chapter 04: sixty-two minutes [SIMULATION]

At ten thirty in the morning, the bomber is about nine hundred and thirty kilometres out and sixty-two minutes from the burst point. [C]

The real bomb fell for about three minutes under its parachute. Our clock leaves that fall out and counts only the bomber's flight. [A4, C]

The next hour is quiet on the map. Nothing in this simulation happens in it that the engine can measure. We do not draw an alert, a warning or a response, because no source we use describes one. [C]

At eleven thirty-two, the clock reads T plus zero. [C]

At the real test, the bomber was about forty kilometres away when the bomb went off, and it was still pushed down by about a thousand metres. [A5]

The light comes first. In nineteen sixty-one, the flash was visible at about a thousand kilometres, even through cloud. [A8]

Then the fireball. In nineteen sixty-one it touched the ground and swelled up to nearly the height of the aircraft that dropped it. [A6]

The heat arrives with the light. At the real test, third-degree burns were possible out to about a hundred kilometres in clear air. Our engine gives a shorter range, and it is coming. [A8, C]

The blast wave follows at the speed of sound, so the farther a ring is from ground zero, the longer it waits. The rings are next. [C]

## Chapter 05: one bomb, ring by ring [SIMULATION]

In our simulation, the fireball has a radius of four point six kilometres, nine point two across. That is wider than Manhattan is wide, and nothing inside it is a building any more. [C]

Start with the centre. Inside the fireball there is no ring to read: nothing is left to measure. [C]

Out to five point one kilometres, the blast is twenty pounds per square inch, a pressure at which published effects models give heavy damage even to strong buildings. [A28, C]

Put that ring on the map. Manhattan is at most about three point seven kilometres wide, so the ring is wider than the island. It takes in the harbour, all of lower Manhattan, and the nearest streets of Brooklyn and New Jersey. [C]

Out to twenty-five kilometres, five pounds per square inch, enough to heavily damage most buildings. The ring covers about two thousand square kilometres in all. [C]

Read it street by street. All of Manhattan is inside it, with the Brooklyn and Jersey waterfronts, and all three big airports. [C]

The five-pound ring is the one to hold in mind. The engine puts its area at about two thousand square kilometres, against about one hundred and twenty-four for the smaller bomb. [C]

Out to fifty-three kilometres, bare skin takes third-degree burns, in clear air. The ring leaves the city and falls over the sea, Long Island, New Jersey and Connecticut. [C]

Out to sixty-seven and a half kilometres, one pound per square inch, the pressure that breaks windows. The engine's rings are conservative: at the real test, glass broke out to about nine hundred kilometres. [A9, C]

Now the same map with a different bomb. This is not a second attack, only a comparison: eight hundred kilotons, the top of Russia's missile warhead range. [A22, C]

Its fireball has a radius of under one kilometre. Its burn ring reaches nine point eight kilometres, and its window ring seventeen. Its five-pound ring reaches six point three kilometres and covers about one hundred and twenty-four square kilometres. [C]

The fifty-megaton five-pound ring reaches almost four times as far, twenty-five kilometres against six, and covers about sixteen times the area. [C]

Each ring of the larger bomb reaches farther than the same ring of the smaller one. Same point, same island, and a bomb that is about sixty times larger. [C]

So where does that leave the map when the light fades? [C]

## Chapter 06: the day after [SIMULATION]

So how far does fifty megatons reach? On the map, the answer is a ring almost twenty-five kilometres in radius, drawn around a single point. In our simulation, the day after begins with that ring going dark. [C]

Inside it, the bridges and tunnels of the harbour are part of the blast area. Published effects models put five pounds per square inch at heavy damage for most ordinary buildings. [C, A28]

Beyond it, the burn ring and the window ring cross the sea into three states. At the real test, windows broke out to about nine hundred kilometres. [A9, C]

Then the wind. Winds here usually blow from the west, so a plume would tend to drift out over Long Island and the sea. That is an assumption of ours. The engine gives little local fallout for an airburst, and we draw no plume. A lower burst, or rain, would change that. The full hundred-megaton design was scrapped in nineteen sixty-one over exactly this fear. [A3, C]

The cloud is a point at the centre of the map. A cloud like the nineteen sixty-one one climbs about sixty-five kilometres, far above where airliners fly. [A7]

Beyond the rings, the map goes quiet. We do not model the power grid, the phones, the hospitals or the response, and no published study we rely on gives a death toll for this strike, so we give none. [C]

The nineteen sixty-one test was over an Arctic range. This map is a city of about eight point six million people, and the rings do not stop at its edge. [A26, C]
