# US versus China: The First Week of a Pacific War, and Who Runs Out of Missiles First

Episode 019. Draft 2, 2026-11-26. About eleven minutes at 138 words per minute. The simulation runs on day numbers only and is tied to no real date. Each paragraph is one narration unit; the tag on a section header is the on-screen corner tag. Refs in brackets are for the fact screener and are not read aloud.

## Cold open [SIMULATION]

Eleven twenty-six, Batan Island: the first nuclear weapon used in war since Nagasaki. [C]

This is a simulation, and it has not happened. [C]

Six minutes earlier, a single missile leaves a launcher in southern China. It is a DF-26. It is aimed at a small airport on an island in the Luzon Strait, where a battery of American launchers sits. [A22, A27, C]

On the sixth day of this war, the question is which side runs short first, and whether it matters. [C]

## Chapter 01: the first four days of the Iran war [REAL DATA]

The story starts in the Middle East. On the twenty-eighth of February, twenty twenty-six, the United States joined Israel's war on Iran. [A2]

RUSI counted more than five thousand munitions fired in the first ninety-six hours, and about eleven thousand three hundred by day sixteen. One estimate puts the cost near twenty-six billion dollars. [A3]

Those are the same kinds of missile a Pacific war would need. CSIS says the prewar stocks were already too small for that fight. That is the war we simulate, and the day six shot at the top of this video belongs to it. [A4, C]

CSIS counted the damage in April. One estimate puts the Tomahawk stock at about three thousand one hundred before the war, with more than a thousand fired. These are outside estimates. Real inventories are classified. [A5, A6]

And as of mid-November, the Iran war is paused and unresolved, with a blockade of Iranian ports still in force. So how much was left for the other ocean? [A2]

## Chapter 02: running short [REAL HISTORY]

This has happened before, at smaller scale. In June twenty eleven, eleven weeks into the Libya air campaign, reports at the time said the American Defence Secretary told NATO that allies were running short of munitions. A small war had already found the limit. [B2]

In August twenty nineteen, the United States left a treaty that banned land-based missiles of this range. Reports at the time say it flew a ground-launched cruise missile more than five hundred kilometres sixteen days later. China, outside the treaty, had already built the largest such force. Now the American launcher Typhon has been sent to the Philippines, and shown in Japan. [B3, A24, A31]

In January twenty twenty-three, CSIS ran a wargame of a Chinese invasion of Taiwan, twenty-four times. The anti-ship missile LRASM ran out in three to seven days. The JASSM-ER, in about thirty. That game is where this simulation takes its shape. [A18, B4]

How fast would it unfold? The missiles fly in minutes. The DF-26 in our simulation takes six. Replacing what is fired takes years: forty-two to sixty-four months, from the day Congress pays. [A11, C]

The Pentagon counted at least three thousand one hundred and fifty Chinese ballistic missiles in twenty twenty-four, about five hundred of them DF-26s. The DF-26 can carry either a conventional or a nuclear warhead. [A21, A22, A27]

The United States makes few. On one estimate, CSIS's five-year average, seventy-nine Tomahawks a year. And ships cannot reload at sea. So what happens when a magazine runs empty? [A11, A33]

## Chapter 03: the first salvo [SIMULATION]

In our simulation, the war is conventional for five days. Every salvo size and every launch site is our choice, not a published figure. [C]

Day one. A DF-17 leaves Guangdong for an American launcher site at Laoag airfield, in northern Luzon. Six minutes. Another missile, a DF-26, goes to the Marine air station at Iwakuni, Japan. Seven minutes. Both launcher sites are on allied soil, which is the point of firing at them. [A23, A31, C]

Less than an hour later, an American B-1B bomber over the Philippine Sea launches an LRASM at a Chinese warship group in the East China Sea. Twenty-eight minutes in flight. It is the American round the simulation will run out of first. In the CSIS wargame that round lasted three to seven days. Watch how long it lasts here. [A13, A18, C]

## Chapter 04: day by day [SIMULATION]

Day two. A guided-missile submarine fires a Tomahawk at an airbase on the Fujian coast. This is a strike on the Chinese mainland. RAND found that target choices like this one move the nuclear threshold. One submarine can carry up to one hundred and fifty-four Tomahawks. [A15, A30, C]

Day three. A DF-26 hits Subic Bay, on Luzon, the port where American ships would reload. The Philippines is an ally of one side, and a target for the other. Every day the war stays conventional, that line gets harder to hold. [A15, C]

By day four, the LRASM is gone. In the wargame it took three to seven days, so this is the fast end of the range, and it is our modelling choice, not a prediction. [A18, C]

Day five. With the anti-ship round empty, a bomber fires a JASSM-ER at a Rocket Force base on the Fujian coast. Forty minutes. In the wargame the JASSM-ER lasted about thirty days. In ours it is only starting to be used, and we do not run it dry. [A18, C]

But the American launchers on allied soil keep firing. They are dispersed and rearmed. More conventional missiles do not stop them. That is our assumption, not a forecast. The war has not ended. It has only stopped depending on who has more missiles in the magazine. [A31, C]

Day six. In our simulation, Beijing decides to break its own no-first-use policy. One DF-26 leaves a simulated site in Guangdong. The warhead is five kilotons, our choice: half of ten kilotons, the level the Pentagon says the PLA is probably pursuing yields below, and about a quarter of Nagasaki. The target is Basco airport, on Batan Island. The day, the target and the weapon are our choices, not a forecast of Chinese policy. [A27, A28, B8, C]

Six minutes. Eleven twenty-six. [C]

## Chapter 05: one warhead, ring by ring [SIMULATION]

The warhead bursts in the air above the airport. Everything below uses the scenario engine. It takes the yield, the weapon class and the height of burst, and returns a radius for each effect. These are rings on a map, not a forecast of what anyone would find. [C]

The fireball has a radius of one hundred and sixteen metres, about the length of a football pitch. Inside it, nothing remains. Five kilotons is small. Nagasaki was twenty-one. [B8, C]

At two hundred and thirty-six metres, twenty pounds per square inch. Any building is destroyed. This is the airport itself, the target the missile was aimed at. [C]

At one point one six kilometres, five pounds per square inch. Houses collapse. The engine draws the ring and stops there. This ring covers four point two square kilometres, and it stops just short of the centre of Basco town. [C]

Third-degree burns reach one point two kilometres, about the same distance. Anyone in the open inside it is burned by the flash itself. The flash arrives before the sound does. [C]

At three point one kilometres, one pound per square inch: windows break, and most of the town of Basco lies inside it. This is the widest ring we draw, and the one where the damage is least. [C]

Because the burst is in the air, there is little local fallout. We give no death toll. The engine does not produce one, and we will not guess. What the rings do show is how small the burst is, and how close it sits to people who are not the target. [C]

## Chapter 06: the day after [SIMULATION]

Back to the question from the top: which side runs short first? By the end of day six, nobody has to be empty for the war to change. One side has used the weapon it promised not to use first, and the counts on the bars no longer decide it. [A28, C]

The cloud first. We assume a wind from the north-east. No one can forecast the real wind. That carries the small cloud south-west, over open sea and the coast of northern Luzon, and we give no dose. [C]

In our simulation, American ships turn toward port. They cannot reload at sea, so every ship that leaves the line to reload is a ship that is not on it, and the port is already a target, as Subic Bay was on day three. [A7, A15, C]

At the factories, nothing changes this week. Rebuilding the stocks takes one to four years. Rocket motors, guidance electronics and energetics are the bottlenecks, and RUSI says China controls more than eighty percent of the world's tungsten. [A4, A14]

The decision belongs to Washington. The options, as we frame them, are three: a reply with a low-yield warhead, reportedly about five kilotons; a conventional reply; or a call. In the real world, Beijing's readout of the nineteenth of November says it proposed a crisis channel. The simulation stops here. [A29, A36, C]

Bloomberg Economics put the cost of a war over Taiwan, a different war from ours, at about ten point six trillion dollars in the first year. That is the study's number, not ours. [A35]

On the map, a ring sits over a small island in the Luzon Strait. The launchers on the other islands are still there, and so is the decision. [C]
