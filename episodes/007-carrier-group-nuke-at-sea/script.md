# One Ten-Kiloton Nuke at Sea, and a US Carrier Strike Group

Episode 007. A labelled simulation of one nuclear burst beside a generic US carrier strike group in the open Pacific, built from Glasstone and Dolan effects scaling, the Navy's own nuclear tests at sea and real Cold War history. No ship, country or date is named. [C]

## Cold open [SIMULATION]

In the middle of the night, open Pacific: a nuclear weapon bursts beside a US carrier strike group. [C]

This is a simulation built from published numbers, and it has not happened. [C]

Twenty minutes earlier, a submarine fires one missile. It is a small weapon, ten kilotons, and it flies low over black water toward a group of ships, one of them about a hundred thousand tons. [A1, C]

The group is steaming in the dark, spread across the sea. Nobody on the bridge knows yet. The question is what one small bomb does to a ship that big, and how far the damage reaches. [C]

## 01 The ships and the test [REAL DATA]

In May nineteen fifty-five, about five hundred miles southwest of San Diego, the Navy hung a thirty-kiloton device on a cable two thousand feet down in deep ocean. Thirty ships and about sixty-eight hundred people took part. It was called Wigwam, and it is the only nuclear test ever fired in deep water. [A28, B3]

Most ships stood five miles upwind. Two heavily shielded ships sat downwind, and the base surge contaminated them, but by one account they stayed under the dose limit. [A29]

That is the question from the first minute: one burst, one group, and what the sea does with it. [A28, C]

A Nimitz-class carrier displaces about one hundred thousand tons and about five thousand people live aboard. Its air wing is sixty aircraft or more. [A1, A2, A3]

It rarely sails alone. A strike group is the carrier plus three to five surface warships and a supply ship, perhaps a submarine. [A4]

An old test shows the scale of what one burst does to ships. [A23]

## 02 How close it has come [REAL HISTORY]

July nineteen forty-six, Bikini. Operation Crossroads used about ninety target ships. The first shot, Able, was a twenty-three-kiloton air burst, and the bomb missed its aim point by roughly seven hundred meters. Five ships still sank. [A22, A23, B1]

Our simulation uses the same kind of miss. A burst that lands beside a ship, not on it. [A23, C]

October nineteen sixty-two, near Cuba. A Soviet submarine, B-59, chased by a US carrier group, carried a ten-kiloton nuclear torpedo. By one account, launch needed three officers, and one of them, Vasily Arkhipov, refused. The boat surfaced. The Americans did not know the torpedo was aboard. [B5]

Ten kilotons is our yield too. [B5, A30]

Then the Cold War ended. In September nineteen ninety-one the US announced it would take every tactical nuclear weapon off its naval forces, and the job was finished in nineteen ninety-two. A carrier group sails today with none, and a new sea-launched nuclear cruise missile is still in development. [B7, A7, A13]

So the group has no nuclear answer on its decks, and the weapon would arrive in about twenty minutes. How would a missile get that far unseen? [A7, C]

## 03 The trigger [SIMULATION]

In our simulation, an unnamed attacker, with no country and no flag, fires one fictional anti-ship cruise missile from a submarine. The missile carries one ten-kiloton warhead. [C]

Ten kilotons is our choice. No published source gives a yield for a modern anti-ship weapon, so we borrowed the size of B-59's torpedo. [A30, B5, C]

The target is a generic group: one carrier, a cruiser, three or four destroyers, a supply ship and a submarine, somewhere in the western Pacific. It is local night. [A4, C]

No real ship is the target, so ours has no name, no hull number and no place on any real calendar. [C]

The burst is set over the lead destroyer of the screen. The carrier is two and a half kilometers away. Both are inputs. Able missed by about seven hundred meters; ours is a larger miss, our choice. [A23, C]

The missile is not yet in the air. What does the group see, and when? [C]

## 04 Minute by minute [SIMULATION]

Minute zero. The submarine launches. The missile settles to a low altitude and flies a little under the speed of sound. [C]

The path is two hundred eighty-four kilometers. At that speed the flight takes twenty minutes. We assume the missile stays unseen long enough that nothing stops it. [C]

Minute twenty point one. The warhead detonates in the air over the lead destroyer. [C]

For a moment the sea is lit from above. The destroyers closest to the centre are inside the fireball and the heaviest rings, and in our simulation they are lost or crippled. The ships at the edge are shaken and still moving. [C]

The first thing aboard the carrier is light. At night, the whole horizon turns white, and the sea itself glows. The blast wave arrives afterward, because it travels slower than light. [C]

In the first seconds, the group goes to general quarters, and the aircraft already aloft are the main striking force left. These are our choices, not a record. [C]

On the flight deck, the problem is simple. Aircraft that are airborne need somewhere to land, and the radar that guides them may be blind for a while. We assume it is. [C]

How much of that reaches the carrier? The answer depends on distance, ring by ring. [C]

## 05 One warhead, ring by ring [SIMULATION]

Start at the centre. The fireball is about one hundred fifty meters in radius, and the lead destroyer is inside it. [C]

At two hundred ninety-seven meters the blast reaches twenty pounds per square inch, heavy blast. At one point four six kilometers it falls to five pounds. That ring covers six point seven square kilometers. [C]

The burn ring for exposed skin reaches one point six kilometers. Escorts inside these rings are the ones at risk of heavy damage. [C]

Look at the scale. A destroyer is a dot inside a ring almost eight kilometers across, so the map draws the ships larger than they are and labels them that way. The rings are drawn exactly as the engine computes them. [C]

The light blast ring, one pound per square inch, reaches three point nine kilometers. The carrier sits at two and a half, between the burn ring and the edge. The model puts it between one and five pounds per square inch, outside the burn ring. [C]

Two limits. The engine draws these rings for land, with no model of a ship, a wave or a hardened hull, and it does not model burst height. So we do not say the carrier survives, and we do not say it sinks. [C]

And this is not Baker. Baker was shallow, underwater and twenty-three kilotons in a lagoon, so its water column does not apply to an air burst. [A24, C]

Count inward from the edge: three point nine kilometers, one point six, one point five, three hundred meters. Every ship near the burst sits somewhere on that target, and distance decides what it feels. [C]

What does the group do when the light fades? [C]

## 06 The day after [SIMULATION]

Back to the first question: what does one small bomb do to a ship that big? On the map, the group stops being a group. The ships turn outward and spread, a dispersal we chose, not a record. [C]

A rescue and medical effort begins from the ships that can still steam. We do not count anyone, and we do not say how many ships that is. [C]

The carrier turns on its counter-measure wash-down, the pipes that spray seawater over deck and hull. The Navy built it for fires and fallout, and nobody knows how it would work after a burst. [A5, C]

The fallout map is nearly empty. The model gives little local fallout for an air burst. At Able, most ships could be reboarded within a day, and at Wigwam, by one account, the ships downwind stayed under the dose limit. Those are two histories, not a forecast. [A23, A29, C]

The reactors would be shut down and checked, an assumption. Radio and radar near the burst may fail, and the air wing may have nowhere safe to land. The engine does not model the electromagnetic pulse, so we claim none. [C]

No published study gives a death toll for a burst like this, so we give none. [C]

Far from the group, the world sees a dark gap on a screen and does not know who fired. On the bridge, someone has to decide what the group does next, with no weapon on the decks to answer and no name to answer. [A7, C]

The sea is quiet again by morning. [C]
