# Oreshnik From Belarus: Eight Minutes to a Nuclear Flash in Germany (Simulation)

Episode 014. Draft 1, 2026-11-21. Target 11 to 13 minutes at 138 words per minute. Each paragraph is one narration unit; the `[TAG]` on a section header is the on-screen corner tag. Source refs in brackets are for the fact screener and are not read aloud.

## Cold open [SIMULATION]

Eighteen minutes past three in the morning, western Germany: a nuclear weapon bursts over Ramstein Air Base. [C]

This is a simulation built from published numbers, and it has not happened. [C]

Eight minutes earlier. A road-mobile launcher stops in a forest in eastern Belarus. No notice is expected. [C, A10, A18]

One missile climbs. In Poland, in Lithuania, in Germany, radars pick up a ballistic track. Nobody on the ground can tell what is on top of it. [A17, C]

How many minutes does Europe get between that launch and the flash, and what can anyone do with them? [C]

## Chapter 01: the missile that flew fifteen minutes [REAL DATA]

On the twenty-first of November, twenty twenty-four, a missile left a Russian test range called Kapustin Yar and came down on a factory area in Dnipro, in Ukraine. Flight time: about fifteen minutes. [A2, B5]

Russia called the missile Oreshnik, the hazel tree. The Pentagon called it an experimental intermediate-range ballistic missile, built on the design of an older intercontinental one. Putin said the warhead was not nuclear. [A1, A2, B5]

That is the missile from the cold open, with one difference. Nobody has ever fired one from Belarus, and nobody has ever fired one at a NATO country. [A1, A8, A13]

Since Dnipro, Russia has used it against Ukraine twice more, in January and May of twenty twenty-six. The January strike landed in the Lviv region, about seventy kilometres from the Polish border. Britain, France and Germany called it escalatory and unacceptable. [A8, A9, A25]

Analysts say the conventional version seems to carry no explosive at all. It does its damage by speed and weight, and its value, they say, is as a signal. [A4, A7]

Its range is roughly three thousand to five thousand kilometres, depending on who is counting. Putin claims its warheads reach Mach 10. Skeptics note that any long-range ballistic missile reaches hypersonic speed in its final fall. Experts quoted by Euronews say its path is predictable, and call it less of a superweapon than Moscow claims. [A5, A6]

In December twenty twenty-five, Russia and Belarus said the system had arrived in Belarus and was on combat duty. Russia has not named the base. Satellite analysts point to a former Soviet airfield near Krichev, five kilometres from the Russian border, and say the launchers drive out on patrol. [A10, A12]

How many are there? At least three launchers, with Minsk talking about up to ten. Moscow has given no count of missiles. [A11]

So what does a base in Belarus change?

## Chapter 02: the last time Europe had minutes [REAL HISTORY]

Less than analysts first assume. Many places in Russia are already closer to London and Paris than that airfield is. What the move shortens is the distance to Lithuania, Poland and Germany. [A14, A16]

We have been here before. In late November nineteen eighty-three, the first American Pershing II missiles went to West Germany. A British parliamentary answer gave a flight time of about fourteen minutes at maximum range. A CIA assessment put the time from first radar detection to impact at eight to ten minutes. [B2]

Soviet leaders feared exactly this: a decapitation strike with almost no warning. This simulation asks the same question, reversed. [B2]

In December nineteen eighty-seven, the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty banned every American and Soviet ground-launched missile with a range of five hundred to five thousand five hundred kilometres, nuclear or conventional. By nineteen ninety-one, two thousand six hundred and ninety-two missiles had been destroyed. [B1]

That ban is gone. The United States left in August twenty nineteen, and Russia followed. In August twenty twenty-five, Russia dropped its own moratorium on deploying such missiles. Six months later, on the fifth of February twenty twenty-six, New START expired. [B3, B4, A22, A23]

For the first time in decades, no treaty limits either side's strategic nuclear forces. Analysts at the Jamestown Foundation note that Belarus is not a party to the notification agreement between Washington and Moscow, so a launch from there has no treaty notice. [A18, B10]

Which raises the question this video exists to answer. If a missile left that forest, how fast would it arrive?

## Chapter 03: the launch [SIMULATION]

In our simulation, a crisis over the Baltic has run for eight days. We do not model how it began, and we do not claim it is likely. On day nine, ten past three in the morning, Berlin time, Moscow fires one missile. [C]

We chose the target: Ramstein Air Base, in Germany, a rear area, far from any front line. We chose the purpose: a nuclear demonstration, to show that the rear can be reached. These are our choices, not a forecast and not a known Russian plan. [C]

The launch site is a point we placed near the reported base, in eastern Belarus. Its location is simulated. [A12, C]

Launch authority stays in Moscow. Analysts at the Jamestown Foundation describe the Belarus battalion as fully subordinate to Russian Strategic Missile Forces. [A13]

The warhead is one warhead. No yield has ever been published for Oreshnik, so we chose fifty kilotons, inside the five to eighty kiloton range of the old Pershing II warhead, used here only as a scale. [A1, B2, C]

A launch like this looks the same on every radar. The Associated Press puts it plainly: there is no way to tell whether a launched Oreshnik carries a nuclear or a conventional warhead before impact. [A17]

So the clock starts, and it starts without an answer.

## Chapter 04: minute by minute [SIMULATION]

Minute zero. The missile leaves the launcher. For the rest of this chapter, the times come from our scenario engine, which assumes a ballistic, not a gliding, path. [C]

If a missile were aimed at Vilnius, it would arrive in about six minutes. Vilnius is four hundred and thirty-three kilometres away. If a missile were aimed at Warsaw, seven hundred and thirty-five kilometres, still about six. Six is the floor of our model. [C]

Berlin, twelve hundred and twenty-six kilometres: about six point seven minutes. Brussels, about eight point six. London, two thousand one hundred and forty-six kilometres: about nine point four. [C]

Ramstein, seventeen hundred and twenty-four kilometres from the launch point, sits between Berlin and Brussels. Minute eight, and a little more. [C]

These are model outputs. The real time depends on a path nobody has published. And the one flight we can measure is slower: Dnipro took about fifteen minutes to cover about eight hundred kilometres, where our model gives six. A higher, lofted path would take longer. [A2, B5, C]

Russian state media claims eleven minutes to an air base in Poland and seventeen to NATO headquarters in Brussels. Those are claims, not measurements. [A15]

What can anyone do with six to nine minutes?

Whether a warning reaches a leader, a commander or a shelter in that time is not established. Whether the American-built missile defence sites at Redzikowo in Poland and Deveselu in Romania could engage it is not publicly established either. They were built against a limited threat from outside Europe, not a Russian barrage. [A19]

The question is what happens at minute eight.

## Chapter 05: one warhead, ring by ring [SIMULATION]

Minute eight point two. One warhead, fifty kilotons, bursts in the air over Ramstein Air Base. [C]

First, the fireball. Its radius is two hundred and ninety metres. This is the fireball ring. [C]

At five hundred and ten metres, twenty pounds per square inch of overpressure. This is the ring of the most severe blast damage. [C]

At two and a half kilometres, five pounds per square inch, enough for heavy damage to ordinary buildings. That ring covers about twenty square kilometres. [C]

At three kilometres, the flash alone causes third-degree burns on bare skin. [C]

At six and three quarter kilometres, one pound per square inch. Windows break. [C]

The base and the towns beside it lie inside these rings. We give no casualty figure: no published study models this strike. [C]

## Chapter 06: the day after [SIMULATION]

The rings are the visible damage. The question from the start was the minutes. [C]

On the map, the rings over Ramstein stay marked. But because the weapon burst in the air, the engine shows little local fallout, so no plume is drawn across Germany. We chose no wind, so we draw none. [C]

Across Europe, the capitals' rings stay on the map: about six minutes for Vilnius and Warsaw, nine for London. Every government now knows what a launch from that forest means, and that it could be a nuclear one. [C, A17]

Germany and its allies have minutes to see the missile, and then days to decide. The only real reaction on record is to the Lviv strike, which Britain, France and Germany called escalatory and unacceptable. That was a conventional strike on Ukraine. [A25, A8]

Belarus is under Russia's nuclear umbrella and hosts, by Lukashenko's account, several dozen Russian tactical nuclear weapons. In our simulation, after this morning, a launch from there reads as a nuclear signal first, and a conventional one only afterward. [A20, A24, C]

And the web that would carry a warning is thin. No INF Treaty, no New START, and, by Jamestown's reading, no launch notice from Belarus. [A18, A22, A23]

The distances on this map are measured from a simulated launch point. The minutes are only a model.
