## Cold open [SIMULATION]

Nine o'clock at night, southern Taiwan: the grid loses ten gigawatts. [C]

This is a simulation built from published numbers, and it has not happened. [A1, C]

Thirty seconds earlier, every light on the island was on. A summer evening, air conditioners running across the island. Then breakers begin to open in Kaohsiung. Nobody at the substations touched them. [C, A15]

By the end of the hour the chip factories that make most of the world's most advanced processors are in trouble. And the question is no longer whether the lights come back. It is how long the machines inside the factories take to start again. [A18, C]

## Chapter 01: an island that cannot make its own power [REAL DATA]

On the third of March, twenty twenty-two, a switch fault at the Hsinta power plant in Kaohsiung tripped a chain of plants across the south. About five and a half million households lost power. Roughly ten and a half gigawatts of supply vanished. [B6]

No one attacked anything. The economics minister later blamed human error and a failed safeguard. [B6]

That is the size of the blackout you just saw. In our simulation, we chose a loss about as big as the 2022 fault, and we ask what happens if the cause is not an accident. [B6, C]

Taiwan imports about ninety-six percent of its energy. Almost all of its natural gas is imported. Gas makes up nearly half of its electricity, and since May twenty twenty-five no nuclear reactor feeds the grid. [A9, A10]

Coal is about a third. The reserve of gas is about eleven days. That is gas only, not electricity. Coal is about forty days, oil about a hundred and forty. [A10, A11, A12]

Taiwan's National Security Bureau says China's cyber army made about two point six million intrusion attempts a day on the island's critical infrastructure in twenty twenty-five. Attempts, not breaches. China denies it. [A5]

As of this video, there is no public evidence that anyone has switched off Taiwan's grid. [A1]

So how do you switch off a grid from a keyboard? Someone already did, in another country. [B1]

## Chapter 02: it has been done, and it was fast [REAL HISTORY]

On the twenty-third of December, twenty fifteen, attackers got inside three regional power companies in Ukraine and opened breakers by remote control. About two hundred and twenty-five thousand customers lost power. [B1]

Then they wiped the control computers and disabled the backup power units, to slow the repair. Operators restored the lights by switching by hand, in one to six hours. [B1]

On the twenty-fourth of February, twenty twenty-two, about an hour before Russia invaded Ukraine, a wiper program bricked the modems of a satellite network called KA-SAT. Ukraine's army lost communications. Remote control of about fifty-eight hundred wind turbines in Germany was cut, though the turbines kept turning. [B3]

They show a pattern: take the power, then the repair, and strike early. [B1, B3]

Taiwan has had a scare of its own. On the third of August, twenty eighteen, a virus spread through TSMC's network after a new tool was plugged in without a scan. Fabs in Tainan, Hsinchu and Taichung were hit, and the most advanced ones took longest to recover. TSMC said it was a failure to follow protocol, not an attack. [B5]

It cost the company about two percent of a quarter's revenue. The fabs were running again within days. That is the benchmark for a mistake. [B5]

A hostile attack would not be a mistake. So how would one begin? [C]

## Chapter 03: hour zero [SIMULATION]

In our simulation, the attackers are already inside. We assume the access was placed years earlier and left to sleep. That is an assumption, not a finding. [C, A5]

There is a precedent for the method. In twenty twenty-four, American agencies warned that a Chinese group called Volt Typhoon had positioned itself inside American energy, communications and water networks, to disrupt them in a crisis. The advisory does not name Taiwan. [A8]

The moment is chosen for harm. It is a summer evening, when demand is well above winter and the night reserve margin, in Taipower's own forecast, is about seven percent. [A15, A16]

At hour zero, malicious commands open breakers at a set of southern substations. Generating units trip behind them. The loss is about ten gigawatts. We chose that figure; it is not a forecast. [C, B6]

Kaohsiung goes first. Then Tainan. Then the dark moves north, city by city, as the grid shakes itself apart to protect itself. [C]

Operators reach for the remote controls and nothing answers. They switch by hand, as the Ukrainians did. And that is slow. [B1, C]

What happens in the next twenty-four hours? [C]

## Chapter 04: the first day [SIMULATION]

Hour one to three. The second wave arrives. Wipers corrupt control systems. Satellite backup terminals are knocked out, the way KA-SAT was. Call-centre lines are flooded, so crews cannot be dispatched. [B1, B3, C]

Two or three undersea cable segments are cut by ships. We choose the number. Real cases are disputed: in twenty twenty-three, two cables to the Matsu Islands were cut within a week by Chinese vessels, and Taiwan's regulator said there was no sign it was intentional. In our simulation, the intent is assumed. [B7, C]

Taiwan reroutes over what is left. The internet slows but does not die. Phones are the weak point, because the towers are on the dark grid. [C]

Hour six. Manual switching has restored about half the lost supply in the north. The south stays in rotating cuts. [C]

Hour twelve. Hospitals and water pumps run on diesel. Fuel exists, but a truck needs a road, a phone and a working signal. [C, A12]

And here is the clock. Our simulation also assumes the gas terminals, which run on power and controls, stall for lack of both. The gas itself is untouched. But about eleven days of it is what the island holds, and it cannot burn what it cannot move. That is our assumption, not a published finding. [A11, C]

Day two and three. Rotating cuts continue. Card payments degrade. In twenty twenty, ransomware at the state oil company CPC knocked out card payments at its gas stations. [B4, C]

Day four to seven. Power returns in pieces. The fabs come back at reduced output, not zero. [C]

Why would the fabs be the slowest to recover? Let us look at one of them. [C]

## Chapter 05: one voltage dip, step by step [SIMULATION]

Take Hsinchu, on the north-west coast. TSMC and other chipmakers run fabs there. [B5, A26, C]

Step one. A dip of a fraction of a second is enough. In April twenty twenty-one, a sudden voltage drop hit TSMC's Nan-ke site in Tainan, and backup systems took over. A split-second dip can scrap wafers in process. [A26]

Step two. Backup systems take over, if they work. In that case, they did. In our simulation, the dip comes with the blackout, and the backups must carry the load alone. [A26, C]

Step three. The power is back, but the network is not. The tools that etch the chips are intact, yet their control systems are corrupted. The restart is set by the network, not the grid. [B5, C]

Step four. With intact tools and a clean network, a fab can restart fast. After the earthquake of April twenty twenty-four, TSMC had more than seventy percent of its machines back online by the end of the same day. [B8]

In our simulation the networks are not clean. So the restart looks closer to twenty eighteen than to twenty twenty-four: days, not hours. [B5, B8, C]

We give no count of wafers lost. No published source supports a count for a blackout like this. [C]

And the world feels it where it is already thin. Taiwan makes most of the world's most advanced chips: about sixty-two percent by one count, ninety-two percent by another, depending on who counts. [A18]

## Chapter 06: the day after [SIMULATION]

Can the island get its lights back before the clock matters? On the map, the answer is a patchwork. By day four, the north glows and the south flickers, block by block, as the operators choose which district waits. [C, A15]

The traffic lights in the dark blocks stay off. Rail runs in sections. That is what a grid operator would have to choose, and we do not know how it would choose. [C]

Is the island cut off? Not quite. Ships still arrive, and by day four the terminals are assumed back on, so the gas clock stops. But the cables are the problem: repairs to the Matsu cables in twenty twenty-three took weeks to months. In our simulation the island lives on what is left of its links. [B7, C]

Where do the chips stop? On the world map, a line leaves Hsinchu and does not arrive. Leading-edge chips for phones, cars and servers are delayed. One study of a blockade, not a blackout, put the economic activity at risk at well over two trillion dollars. We do not apply that figure here. [A18, A19, C]

How does the world respond? No published model covers a Taiwan blackout, and the CSIS blockade wargames did not model cyber. We do not know. [A14, C]

What we do know is smaller and quieter. A grid can fall over without a missile. It has before, in Ukraine, and in Taiwan itself. This did not happen. The lights are on. [A1, B1, B6]
