# One Warhead Hits Washington: Where the Government Goes in 30 Minutes (Simulation)

Episode 011. Draft 1, 2026-11-18. Target about 12 minutes at 138 words per minute. Each paragraph is one narration unit; the tag on a section header is the on-screen corner tag. Refs in brackets trace to research.md and are not read aloud.

## Cold open [SIMULATION]

Ten eleven, Washington: one nuclear warhead bursts above the White House. [C]

This is a simulation, and it has not happened. [A1]

Eleven minutes earlier, a submarine in the North Atlantic fires one missile. Satellites detect the launch. Analysts estimate the president would have less than ten minutes to decide what to do. [A7, A8, A10, C]

## Chapter 01: the day the government left [REAL DATA]

On the morning of September eleventh, two thousand and one, the Vice President was taken below the White House to a bunker, within minutes of the South Tower being hit. [A20, B8]

The President was in the air, aboard Air Force One, with limited communications. The Defense Secretary was at the Pentagon crash site, out of reach for much of the crisis. [B8]

At about ten to ten, military helicopters flew congressional leaders to a mountain bunker in Virginia, called Mount Weather. They stayed until late afternoon. [A21, B9]

And for the first time, the government quietly activated its continuity plans: dozens, perhaps a hundred and fifty officials, rotating through two bunkers outside the city. The public learned of it the following March. [A20]

That was a morning of attacks you could watch unfold, minutes at a time. In our simulation, the warning from a submarine is eleven minutes. [A20, C]

What does the government actually move toward, and how far away is it? [A13, A14]

## Chapter 02: how close, how fast [REAL HISTORY]

Mount Weather is about fifty to sixty miles west of the White House, the home of FEMA's command centre. Raven Rock, in Pennsylvania, about sixty miles north, is the Pentagon's emergency home, built in the early nineteen fifties and nicknamed the underground Pentagon. [A13, A14]

In nineteen fifty-nine to nineteen sixty-two, the government built a third site for all of Congress under a hotel wing at the Greenbrier resort in West Virginia, about three hundred kilometres away. It stayed secret for thirty years. The Washington Post exposed it in nineteen ninety-two, and it was closed at once. It was never needed. The whole idea, a government that walks away from its capital, is as old as the hydrogen bomb. [A23, B1, B3]

In November nineteen seventy-nine, a training tape loaded into a live warning system showed an attack that was not there. The flying command post took off. In June nineteen eighty, a second false alarm told the national security adviser that two thousand two hundred Soviet missiles were in the air. He was about a minute from calling the president when the warning proved false. [B5, B6]

Both times, the cause was a computer, not an enemy. Both times, the clock was the problem. People were deciding in minutes, on a screen, with nothing to check it against. [B5, B6]

From a silo, a missile takes about thirty minutes. From a submarine offshore, as little as ten to fifteen. Analysts estimate that the president would have less than ten minutes to decide. That is an analyst's estimate, not an official figure. [A7, A8]

This is the question every one of those sites was built to answer, and none of them has ever faced a nuclear attack. So what happens in those first minutes? [C]

## Chapter 03: the trigger [SIMULATION]

In our simulation, a single missile leaves a submarine in the open North Atlantic. We choose the launch point, and we name no attacker. [C]

It carries one warhead. We choose a yield of one hundred kilotons, detonating in the air over central Washington. We choose ten o'clock in the morning. The engine puts the flight at eleven minutes. [C, A7]

Why one warhead, when real plans use many? No published plan describes a single strike on Washington. We choose it because it is the smallest case that lets us watch the response, instead of a war. [C]

What the president orders back is not part of this simulation. We follow the government's movement only, and we stop at the flash.

Satellites see the launch. Radars and a second kind of sensor confirm it, the rule called dual phenomenology. The nuclear command and control network is about two hundred and fifty ground, space and airborne systems, by one Congressional Research Service count, and every minute the warning takes comes out of the eleven. [A10, C]

The first question is who moves, and how fast. [C]

## Chapter 04: minute by minute [SIMULATION]

Minute zero, ten o'clock. The launch is detected by satellites in space, and the alert goes up the chain. Every step after this is a choice we make, and not a documented schedule. [A10, C]

Minute two. Officers assess the warning against a second sensor. They have no time for a long review, and the president joins an emergency conference with senior officials. The authority to order a nuclear strike belongs to the president alone. Neither the military nor Congress can overrule it. [A8, A9, C]

Minute four. The Secret Service takes the president out of the building. The codes that authenticate an order travel in a case called the football. In this simulation, the president heads for an aircraft rather than a bunker under the city. That is our choice, not a documented plan. [A9, C]

Minute five. The flying command post, a modified Boeing seven forty-seven called the E-4B, is not on the spot. It is based at Offutt, Nebraska, sixteen hundred kilometres away. It can carry about a hundred people. It is built to stay in the air for about a week, with refuelling, and its systems are shielded against electromagnetic pulse. It cannot be there in eleven minutes, so in this simulation the president needs some other aircraft first. [A11]

Minute six. In this simulation, congressional leaders are flown toward Mount Weather, as on September eleventh. Raven Rock and Mount Weather are activated. [A13, A14, A21, C]

Minute eight. The Pentagon, the Capitol and the cabinet offices each count who has left and who has not. In this simulation, many have not. We do not model who. [C]

Minute ten. About one minute is left on the clock. A president who has reached an aircraft can still give orders from it. What those orders are is outside the simulation. [A9, C]

Minute eleven. Impact. [A7, C]

Next: what one hundred kilotons does, ring by ring. [C]

## Chapter 05: one warhead, ring by ring [SIMULATION]

All numbers here come from our scenario engine. They are estimates for a labelled simulation. [C]

The fireball is about three hundred and eighty metres in radius, almost eight hundred metres across. It sits above the White House and the ground around it. [C]

Out to six hundred and forty metres, the blast adds twenty pounds per square inch of pressure. Almost nothing stays standing. [C]

Out to three point one kilometres, a pressure of five pounds per square inch wrecks most buildings. That is thirty-one square kilometres. The Capitol, two and a half kilometres from the White House, is inside the ring. The Pentagon, three point three kilometres away, sits at its edge. The engine's uncertainty is bigger than that gap, so at the edge never means safe. [A16, C]

The flash burns bare skin to four point two kilometres. [C]

At eight and a half kilometres, windows break and glass flies. [C]

For an air burst, the engine says there is little local fallout. That is the honest picture: the damage here is mostly blast and heat, close to the centre. [C]

Mount Weather, seventy-six kilometres away, and Raven Rock, ninety-nine, are far outside every ring. In the engine, they take no blast. That is a result for this yield, and no guarantee for any bunker. [A13, A14, C]

## Chapter 06: the day after [SIMULATION]

Who is in charge at ten twelve? The answer depends on who had already left the city, and on what the map shows once the rings are drawn. Start with the map. [C]

Inside three kilometres of the White House, the centre of the federal government is dark. In our simulation, the rings cover the Capitol, the Mall and the executive offices. Out to eight and a half kilometres, windows break. [A16, C]

The plume is small. With a west-northwest wind, our assumption, it drifts east-southeast toward the Chesapeake Bay. For an air burst, the engine's fallout is little local fallout, so the lasting damage is rubble, fire and closed bridges. [C]

Two points stay lit outside the rings: Mount Weather and Raven Rock. Whoever reached them, the law already lists who acts next: after the president and vice president, the Speaker of the House, then the president pro tempore of the Senate, then the cabinet by office, seventeen in all. Who holds each office is not modelled. [A17, C]

The hard gap is Congress. The House can refill its seats only by special election, which takes states about four months on average. Governors can fill Senate seats within days. Whether a quorum counts the living or the whole chamber is a question scholars dispute. In two thousand and three, a commission imagined a nuclear attack in which Congress had been annihilated, and urged a constitutional amendment. [A18]

And one aircraft is aloft: the one the president boarded. The E-4B has stayed up for thirty-five hours in a test, and it is designed for about a week. Whether it reaches them is not modelled. That is all the map can say. [A11, C]
