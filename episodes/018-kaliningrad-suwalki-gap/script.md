# What If NATO Cut Off Kaliningrad, and Russia Fired a Nuclear Warning on Its Own Soil

Episode 018. Draft 1, 2026-11-25. Target 11 to 13 minutes at about 138 words per minute. Each paragraph is one narration unit; the `[TAG]` on a section header is the on-screen corner tag for everything under it until the next header. Facts trace to research.md (A, B and C numbers in brackets are for the fact screener and are not read aloud).

## Cold open [SIMULATION]

Six thirteen in the evening, Kaliningrad: the first nuclear weapon used since Nagasaki. This is a simulation, and it has not happened. [A1, C]

Three minutes earlier. A missile leaves a launcher in the east of the enclave. It is an Iskander, and it flies ninety-eight kilometres. [C]

Its target is open ground chosen by the simulation, inside Russia itself. [C]

Why would Russia fire a nuclear weapon at its own land? And what does NATO do in the hour after? Thirteen days of pressure lead to this minute, and the answer starts with a road. [C]

## Chapter 01: a place with no road home [REAL DATA]

On the thirtieth of September, twenty twenty-six, Russia's embassy in Dublin said Russia would use "all its arsenal, including nuclear weapons" if NATO tried to isolate Kaliningrad. Reuters reported the same warning in a formal note to NATO states. [A2]

NATO's secretary general answered: "stop the nuclear threats." NATO's top commander said the alliance has "no need and incentive to escalate." NATO denies any plan to move against the enclave. [A2]

So what is Kaliningrad? A patch of Russia about the size of Connecticut, with about one million people, squeezed between Poland and Lithuania. It has no land link to the rest of Russia. [A9]

Its trains cross about one hundred and fifty miles of Lithuania, by one account. Since February twenty twenty-five, it has no electricity link to Russia either. Its port, Baltiysk, is Russia's only Baltic port that stays ice-free in winter. [A9, A22, B8]

The enclave holds the Baltic Fleet's headquarters and a dense belt of air defence and missiles. That includes an Iskander brigade, there permanently since twenty eighteen. The missile can carry a conventional or a nuclear warhead. [A10, A11, B5]

On the other side is a strip of land about sixty-five kilometres wide, the Suwalki gap, between Kaliningrad and Belarus. It holds the only land route to Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania: two main roads and one single-track rail line. [A6, A7]

What happens if someone closes the road into Kaliningrad instead? It has been tried before. [B2]

## Chapter 02: how close it has come [REAL HISTORY]

In June nineteen forty-eight, Soviet forces closed the road, rail and water routes into the Allied sectors of West Berlin. The airlift kept the city supplied, at one point with a plane landing about every forty-five seconds. The blockade was lifted eleven months later. In our simulation, the closed enclave is Russian. [B2]

On the eighteenth of June, twenty twenty-two, Lithuania stopped sanctioned goods moving by rail to Kaliningrad. The governor said it hit forty to fifty percent of the goods the enclave moves through Lithuania. Moscow called it openly hostile, and a Russian lawmaker suggested force could be used. Lithuania lifted the rail ban five weeks later, and no shots were fired. Our simulation makes that ban total. [B7]

In July twenty twenty-five, the commander of US Army Europe and Africa said NATO could take control of Kaliningrad "in a timeframe that is unheard of." One report says a Russian lawmaker answered that any attack would bring retaliation, including nuclear weapons. It was one general's remark, not NATO policy. Both sides have already said uncomfortable things about this enclave. [B9]

How fast could it unfold? In our simulation, thirteen days from the first closed rail line to one flash. The last missile flies for three minutes. How does it start? [C]

## Chapter 03: the trigger [SIMULATION]

In our simulation, day one begins with a decision. NATO states halt all rail and road transit to Kaliningrad through Lithuania. It is modelled on the twenty twenty-two ban, but total this time. The simulation assumes it, and NATO has not planned it. [A2, B7, C]

The enclave cannot lean on its neighbours. The Baltic states left the Russian grid in twenty twenty-five, so Kaliningrad runs its own power. Its gas, its goods and its soldiers' supplies now need the sea. Russia's own cost of supplying the enclave is about six hundred and forty-seven million dollars a year, by one estimate. [A22, B8, C]

Days two to seven are pressure, not yet war. Neither side fires, and both sides count what they have left. The simulation assumes a maritime inspection line off Baltiysk. Russia answers with ferries from Saint Petersburg, with jamming, and with harassment of undersea cables. Moscow sends the note it sent in real life. [A13, A22, A2, C]

Each side watches the other's ships, and each side reads the other's silence. Planners in both capitals know the old precedent, that a blockade can end without a shot. Nobody in the simulation wants the next step, and everybody can see it coming. Every day the inspection line holds, the pressure on the enclave grows. By day eight, the line is no longer a line on paper. What does the first shot look like? [C]

## Chapter 04: day by day [SIMULATION]

Day eight. The simulation assumes a Polish strike package launches one conventional cruise missile at Baltiysk, the base that guards the inspection line. It flies one hundred and twenty-two kilometres in about nine minutes. The shooting line is crossed, and no one on either side can say it was an accident. [C]

Russia's reply is not instant. For three days, the note, the ferries and the jammers are all it uses. In the simulation, Moscow wants the alliance to believe that the next answer will come, and to wonder what it will be. [C]

Day eleven. Russia answers. One conventional Iskander flies a hundred kilometres in three minutes, to the rail and road junction at Marijampole, on the Via Baltica in Lithuania. The Iskander reaches up to about five hundred kilometres by one estimate, enough for Warsaw, Vilnius and Riga. A Russian strike on a Lithuanian town is the simulation's assumption, and it puts Article Five in question at once. No death toll is computed for it. [A7, A11, C]

Day twelve. In the simulation, a German brigade and an allied battlegroup move toward the corridor from Lithuania. In Belarus, forces mobilise at Grodno, about seventy-five kilometres from Suwalki. The sixty-five kilometre gap now has two armies on its edges, and two roads and one rail line between them. This is assumed. [A7, A17, A20, C]

Day thirteen. Moscow argues that a threat to Kaliningrad is a conventional threat to its territory, the fourth condition in its own doctrine. Analysts differ on whether an isolation meets it. The simulation chooses a warning shot on Russian soil, fired at the one place where it can harm no ally. [A18, C]

At ten past six in the evening, a launcher in the enclave raises its missile. Three minutes later the flash comes. One missile. What does one warhead do, ring by ring? [C]

## Chapter 05: one warhead, ring by ring [SIMULATION]

The Iskander-M can carry a nuclear warhead, but its yield is not published. The simulation assumes five kilotons, about a quarter of Nagasaki. It bursts in the air, above open forest and farmland in the east of the enclave, ninety-eight kilometres from its launcher. [A11, C]

The fireball reaches one hundred and sixteen metres from the burst point at most. Inside two hundred and thirty-six metres, pressure passes twenty pounds per square inch. [C]

Out to one point one six kilometres, pressure passes five pounds per square inch. That ring covers four point two square kilometres. [C]

Third-degree burns reach one point two kilometres. Windows break out to three point one kilometres. [C]

The engine finds little local fallout. The simulation places the burst over open forest and farmland. The point of this weapon is not the damage. It is the message, with a flight time of three minutes. [C]

So the rings are small, and the flash is over. What does the map look like after it? [C]

## Chapter 06: the day after [SIMULATION]

Back to the question: what does NATO do in the hour after? Start with the sky. The simulation assumes a west wind of about five metres a second, not a forecast, so a thin cloud drifts east over Kaliningrad's own countryside toward the Lithuanian border. Little local fallout, and no dose map. [C]

Draw a circle of three hundred and fifty kilometres around the enclave, the reach of its military jammer, according to the Kyiv Independent. Warsaw, Gdansk and Vilnius are inside it. In the simulation, airlines reroute around the Baltic, and the circle on the map stays nearly empty of flight tracks. [A8, A13, C]

The corridor changes next. Two roads and one single-track rail line were already the only link to the Baltic states. In the simulation, planners treat all three as possible targets, and every convoy now has to choose between them. [A7, C]

Kaliningrad itself is quieter and more alone. Its ferries and its own power grid are all it has, and the world now watches every one of them. The power grid runs without any link to Russia. [A9, A22, C]

And Lithuania. Its parliament has passed the first of three votes to repeal its constitutional ban on hosting nuclear weapons. After this, the last vote would read differently. [A5, C]

The simulation stops here, at the decision. The real options are a reply in kind or a conventional reply, an alliance meeting, or a pause for talks. Whether a burst on Russian soil triggers Article Five is the question the simulation leaves open. [A18, C]

The ban on transit is still in force, and the gap is still two roads and one rail line. [A7, C]
