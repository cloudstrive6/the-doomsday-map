# Russia Launches a Nuclear Strike on America... What Happens Next?

Episode 001. Draft 1, 2026-11-05. Target 12 to 13 minutes at about 135 words per minute. Each paragraph is one narration unit; the `[TAG]` on a section header is the on-screen corner tag for everything under it until the next header. Facts trace to research.md (A, B and C numbers in brackets are for the fact screener and are not read aloud).

## Cold open [SIMULATION]

Twenty to four in the morning, Nebraska. Most of America is asleep.

Eight thousand kilometres away, in a forest south of Moscow, a silo lid slides open. Then another. Within ninety seconds, more than three hundred missiles are climbing out of Russia. In twenty-five minutes, the first of them will arrive.

What you are about to watch is a simulation. It has not happened. But the missiles are real, the bases are real, the timings come from the people who study this for a living, and every number on screen has a source. This is what a Russian first strike on America would look like, minute by minute.

## Chapter 01: the real forces [REAL DATA]

Start with what exists today. Russia and the United States hold almost nine in every ten nuclear warheads on Earth. About twelve thousand in total. Of those, each side keeps roughly seventeen hundred strategic warheads deployed, mounted on missiles and bombers, ready to use. [A1]

Russia's land-based force is spread across eleven missile divisions, from Kozelsk and Tatishchevo in the west to Irkutsk in Siberia. About three hundred and thirty intercontinental missiles. The newest, the Yars, carries several warheads each. The heaviest, the Sarmat, is still being installed in converted silos at Uzhur. [A2]

At sea, Russia's Borei submarines carry sixteen Bulava missiles apiece, each with a reach of about eight thousand kilometres. They sail from two harbours: Gadzhiyevo, near Murmansk in the Arctic, and Vilyuchinsk, on the Kamchatka peninsula in the Pacific. [A3]

America's answer sits under the northern plains. Four hundred Minuteman missiles in four hundred and fifty silos, spread across Montana, North Dakota, Wyoming, Colorado and Nebraska, each carrying a warhead of about three hundred kilotons. Twenty times Hiroshima, in a hole in a wheat field. [A4, A5]

And one city ties the whole American system together. Bellevue, Nebraska, just south of Omaha. Offutt Air Force Base. For forty years it was the headquarters of Strategic Air Command, which built its command post underground to survive a Soviet strike. Today it is the headquarters of United States Strategic Command, the people who would carry out a nuclear order. It has been on the other side's maps since the nineteen fifties. [A7]

## Chapter 02: thirty minutes [REAL DATA]

Here is the arithmetic that governs everything. An intercontinental missile fired from Russia reaches the United States in about thirty minutes. [A6]

American infrared satellites see the launch within seconds. The data goes to NORAD under the Rockies, to Strategic Command at Offutt, and to the National Military Command Center in the Pentagon. [A6]

Ground radars in Greenland, Alaska, England and on both American coasts confirm it as the missiles rise over the horizon. That takes about ten minutes. By the time the president is on the line, there may be less than ten minutes to decide whether to fire America's own missiles before they are destroyed in their silos. Analysts who have studied the procedure put the real decision window at about six minutes. [A6]

Nobody has ever had to make that decision for real. Our simulation asks what the thirty minutes look like if they ever do.

## Chapter 03: the warning shot that was never answered [SIMULATION]

Imagine the war in Ukraine spreads north. A clash on NATO's eastern flank goes badly for Russia, and Moscow believes an attack on Russian territory is coming. The doctrine it rewrote in twenty twenty-four says a nuclear answer is on the table. Instead of one warning shot, Russia's leadership makes the oldest calculation in nuclear strategy: if war is coming, strike the other side's weapons before they can be used. [B5]

In our scenario, the first wave is about six hundred and fifty warheads aimed at some five hundred targets. Two for every Minuteman silo. The bomber bases. The two submarine harbours. The early-warning radars. And the command posts: the Pentagon, Cheyenne Mountain, and Offutt. The numbers are ours, built from open sources. They are a scenario, not a forecast.

## Chapter 04: minute by minute [SIMULATION]

Minute zero. Three forty in the morning, Central time. The first silos open at Kozelsk, Tatishchevo, Dombarovsky, Uzhur, Yoshkar-Ola, Teykovo, Nizhny Tagil, Novosibirsk, Barnaul and Irkutsk. In the Barents Sea and the Sea of Okhotsk, submarines fire from under the ice and the swell.

Minute one. Infrared satellites over the Pacific and the Indian Ocean register hundreds of launch flares at once. At Buckley, in Colorado, the crew sees the display fill up. The first phone call is to NORAD.

Minute three. NORAD's assessment: this is not a test, not a glitch, not a single missile. It is a full launch. The National Military Command Center opens a conference. Strategic Command at Offutt opens its own. The president is woken.

Minute eight. The radar at Pituffik, in northern Greenland, picks up the first missiles climbing over the pole. The satellites were right.

Minute twelve. The first warhead of the war explodes. It is not aimed at a city. It is aimed at that radar in Greenland, a submarine shot with a short flight, to blind the system before the main wave arrives.

Minute thirteen. The president is on the conference. The briefing is short, because it has been rehearsed for decades. The options are on a card. The question is whether to launch the Minuteman force now, while it still exists.

Minute nineteen. The order is given. In our simulation, the United States launches under attack. Four hundred missiles come out of the plains, heading north over the pole in the opposite direction. The two salvos pass each other somewhere over the Arctic.

Minute twenty. Bangor, Washington, home port of the Pacific submarine fleet. Two ground bursts on the piers. Any submarine still tied up is gone. The ones at sea are not, which is the point of submarines.

Minute twenty-two. Washington. Six warheads, airburst, over the Pentagon and the capital. The government has already left, by helicopter, for the alternate command posts. The city has not.

Minute twenty-five. The silo fields. Minot, then Malmstrom, then F.E. Warren. Hundreds of ground bursts across the plains, two on each silo, at three hundred and eight hundred kilotons. Most of the silos are already empty. The craters are not.

Minute twenty-seven. Offutt.

## Chapter 05: one warhead [SIMULATION]

Take that one warhead. Eight hundred kilotons, a ground burst, because the target is buried. The fireball is more than two kilometres across. Everything inside a kilometre of the runway is simply gone. [A12, C]

To five kilometres, most buildings are destroyed. That is the whole of Bellevue, a city of sixty thousand people, and the base itself.

To nearly ten kilometres, anyone outdoors suffers third-degree burns from the flash alone. That reaches the southern suburbs of Omaha.

To fourteen kilometres, windows blow in. Downtown Omaha is fifteen kilometres from the runway. It keeps its windows, barely, and loses its power, its water pressure and its hospitals' capacity in the same minute.

And because this one burst was on the ground, it throws thousands of tonnes of irradiated soil into the cloud. The wind in November blows from the west. The fallout drifts over Council Bluffs and across Iowa through the morning. [A13]

## Chapter 06: the day after [REAL DATA]

The rule that saves lives in fallout is seven to ten: seven hours after a burst, the radiation is a tenth of what it was; after two days, a hundredth. Get inside, into a basement or the middle of a large building, and stay there. [A13]

In the United States there is no national shelter system to go to. The Cold War programme was allowed to lapse decades ago. What remains is the advice: get inside, stay inside, stay tuned.

Scale this up to the whole exchange. Princeton University's Plan A simulation, built from real war plans and real targets, counts thirty-four million dead and fifty-seven million injured within the first few hours of a US-Russia war, before fallout and before the hospitals fail. [A8]

Then the smoke. Burning cities would loft about a hundred and fifty million tonnes of soot above the weather. A study in the journal Nature Food in twenty twenty-two estimated that a war of this size would leave more than five billion people starving within two years. Not from the bombs. From the harvests that never come. [A9]

## Chapter 07: how close it has come [REAL HISTORY]

None of this has happened. The system has come close.

On the ninth of November nineteen seventy-nine, a training tape loaded into a NORAD computer showed a massive Soviet attack under way. The national security adviser was woken and told. He was minutes from calling the president when it was found to be false. [B1]

In June nineteen eighty, a single faulty computer chip, worth forty-six cents, wrote random numbers into the missile-warning messages. Twice in a week, the screens showed an attack. [B2]

In September nineteen eighty-three, a Soviet officer named Stanislav Petrov saw five American missiles on his satellite display and decided it was an error. He was right. [B3]

And in nineteen ninety-five, a Norwegian science rocket was read by Russian radar as a possible missile. President Yeltsin's nuclear briefcase was opened. [B4]

## Chapter 08: the clock [REAL DATA]

Today there are more than twelve thousand nuclear warheads in the world. In January twenty twenty-six, the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists set the Doomsday Clock to eighty-five seconds to midnight, the closest it has ever been. [A1, A10]

Nine days later, on the fifth of February, New START, the last treaty limiting American and Russian strategic weapons, expired. For the first time in half a century, there is no limit at all. [A11]

For eighty-one years, every person with the authority to launch has decided not to. The thirty minutes you just watched exist only on this map.

The only way to keep them there is for the first decision, the one at minute zero, never to be made.
