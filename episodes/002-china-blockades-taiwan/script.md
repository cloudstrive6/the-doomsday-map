# China Blockades Taiwan... and the First Nuke Since 1945 Is Fired

## Cold open [SIMULATION]

Day one. Five in the morning, sixty kilometres off Kaohsiung, Taiwan's biggest port.

A gas tanker hears a call on the radio, in English and in Mandarin. China Coast Guard. Stop your engines. Prepare to be boarded for inspection. Within the hour, the same call goes out to every ship heading for Taiwan.

Taiwan has about eleven days of gas. On day twenty-three, for the first time since Nagasaki, a nuclear weapon is used in war.

What you are about to watch is a simulation. It has not happened. But the coast guard patrols that begin it are real: China started them east of Taiwan in October twenty twenty-six. Every number on screen has a source. This is how a blockade of Taiwan could end with the first nuclear shot in eighty-one years, day by day. [A2]

## Chapter 01: an island that runs on ships [REAL DATA]

Taiwan imports about ninety-seven percent of its energy, and almost all of it arrives by sea. [A13]

Nearly half of its electricity comes from natural gas, almost all of it shipped in as liquid through three terminals: one in Kaohsiung, one in Taichung, one near Taipei. Its last nuclear reactor shut down in May twenty twenty-five. [A12, A14]

By law, Taiwan keeps about eleven days of gas in storage. Coal, about forty days. Oil, about four and a half months. [A15]

It grows only about thirty percent of the calories its people eat. [A17]

And what leaves the island matters as much as what arrives. Taiwan makes most of the world's most advanced computer chips. More than a fifth of all the world's seaborne trade, about two and a half trillion dollars of it, sails through the strait beside it. [A18, A19]

Across that strait, about a hundred and thirty kilometres away at its narrowest, is China. [A8]

## Chapter 02: it has already started [REAL HISTORY]

China has been rehearsing this for thirty years.

In March nineteen ninety-six, two weeks before Taiwan's first direct presidential election, China fired missiles into the sea off Keelung and Kaohsiung, Taiwan's two biggest ports. America sent two aircraft carriers. [B3]

In August twenty twenty-two, after a visit by the Speaker of the US House, Nancy Pelosi, China fired eleven ballistic missiles into the waters around Taiwan. Five landed inside Japan's economic zone. [B4]

In October twenty twenty-four, a record one hundred and fifty-three Chinese military aircraft in a single day, with coast guard ships circling the island. [B5]

In April twenty twenty-five, an exercise China openly called training in blockade and control, with live fire at simulated ports and energy facilities. [B6]

In December twenty twenty-five, rockets fired into zones north and south-west of Taiwan, some of them overlapping Taiwan's own territorial waters for the first time since twenty twenty-two. [B7]

And in October twenty twenty-six, something new. China's coast guard said it was strengthening control over the waters east of Taiwan, and began patrolling them, broadcasting that it was enforcing the law. Taiwan calls it practice for a blockade. [A2]

## Chapter 03: quarantine, not blockade [REAL DATA]

Why the coast guard, and not the navy? Because of one word.

Under international law, a blockade of a country's ports by armed forces is an act of aggression. It can mark the start of a war. A quarantine, run by coast guards and customs officers, is, at least on paper, something less. When America surrounded Cuba with warships in nineteen sixty-two, it chose the word quarantine for the same reason. [A35]

China has the largest coast guard in the world, with more than a hundred and fifty ocean-going ships. Taiwan's has about ten. [A23]

## Chapter 04: day by day [SIMULATION]

Day one. Beijing announces that every ship bound for Taiwan must stop for customs inspection. More than three dozen coast guard ships take up positions off the ports. The navy stays over the horizon. [A35, C]

Day two. Most shipping companies do what their insurers tell them. They wait. Tankers slow down, or turn around.

Day four. Taiwan cuts gas to heavy industry, to keep the lights on in homes and hospitals.

Day six. A Taiwanese warship tries to escort a gas tanker into Kaohsiung. A Chinese cutter blocks its bow. Nobody fires. That night, Beijing declares closure zones around Kaohsiung, Keelung, Taichung and the gas terminals. The quarantine is now a blockade, and it is enforced by warships. [B7, C]

Day nine. Rolling blackouts across Taiwan. The chip factories are on priority power, but carmakers and phone makers around the world are already counting their stock.

Day eleven. The gas line on the chart reaches zero. In the CSIS blockade wargames, this is about where it ran out too. Close to half of Taiwan's electricity is gone. Keeping the island supplied by air would take more than eight hundred cargo flights a day. [A15, A16]

Day fourteen. America and Japan announce a convoy: gas tankers from the south, through the Bashi Channel, escorted by American destroyers. [C]

Day sixteen. The convoy reaches the closure zone. A Chinese destroyer fires on an escort. Within hours, China strikes the American air bases at Kadena, on Okinawa, and on Guam, with conventional missiles. A hypersonic missile from Fujian reaches Okinawa in about seven minutes. Guam, in about thirteen. [A24, A26, A37, C]

Day nineteen. American submarines go to work. In the CSIS wargames of an invasion, China lost more than a hundred and thirty major ships. Here, the blockade fleet starts to thin. [A33, C]

Day twenty-two. The blockade is failing. For a leadership in Beijing that has just purged its own top generals, a failed blockade is not a lost battle. It is a threat to the party itself. [A6, C]

And that, the Pentagon says, is the one situation in which China would probably consider using a nuclear weapon first. [A29]

## Chapter 05: day twenty-three [SIMULATION]

Four ten in the morning, Taipei time. A road-mobile launcher in southern China fires a single DF-26. The same launchers carry conventional warheads or nuclear ones. Nobody watching on radar can tell which this is. [A30]

Its target is not a city. It is the American carrier group in the Philippine Sea, about six hundred and fifty kilometres east of Taiwan. [C]

Seven minutes later, the warhead detonates a few hundred metres above the sea. Ten kilotons: the top of the Pentagon's estimate for China's smallest warheads, and about half of Nagasaki. [A29, B8, C]

The fireball is about three hundred metres across.

Within about one and a half kilometres, a blast like this would flatten most buildings on land. At sea, that ring holds the carrier.

Within one point six kilometres, the flash alone burns anyone out on deck.

Out to about four kilometres, it would shatter windows on land. The ships further out are shaken, and still afloat. [C]

For the first time in eighty-one years, a nuclear weapon has been used in war.

## Chapter 06: the bill [REAL DATA]

The world would be paying long before the flash. Bloomberg Economics estimates a one-year blockade of Taiwan would cut about five percent from the world economy. A war over Taiwan, nearly ten percent: more than ten trillion dollars in the first year. [A21]

In that war, Taiwan's economy shrinks by forty percent. South Korea's by twenty-three. Japan's by fifteen. America's by almost seven. [A21]

Those numbers do not include a nuclear weapon.

## Chapter 07: it has been planned before [REAL HISTORY]

The last time America faced China across this strait, it was America planning nuclear strikes.

In nineteen fifty-five, during the first Taiwan Strait crisis, President Eisenhower said tactical atomic weapons could be used on military targets just as you would use a bullet. [B1]

In nineteen fifty-eight, China shelled the island of Kinmen and tried to starve it out with a blockade. America's top general said that if nuclear strikes on Chinese air bases failed, there would be no alternative but nuclear strikes deep into China, as far north as Shanghai, even at the risk of Soviet retaliation. Eisenhower escorted the supply ships instead, and the crisis passed. [B2]

## Chapter 08: the decision [REAL DATA]

China says it will never be the first to use nuclear weapons, at any time, under any circumstances. It has about six hundred and twenty warheads, most of them in storage, and the Pentagon expects more than a thousand by twenty thirty. [A28, A29]

America has never made that promise. On its submarines it carries a small number of low-yield warheads, about five kilotons each, built for a limited nuclear war. [A31, A32]

So on the morning of day twenty-three, the card in front of the American president has three options. Answer with a small nuclear weapon. Answer without one. Or call Beijing.

In December twenty twenty-four, CSIS and MIT played out a nuclear war over Taiwan fifteen times. In three of them, the decisions that followed destroyed cities and killed millions. And what worked better than threats at stopping China from gambling on a nuclear weapon was diplomacy. [A36]

Our simulation stops at that decision, because nobody can tell you how it ends.

The coast guard ships at the start of this video are not part of the simulation. They are there now.
