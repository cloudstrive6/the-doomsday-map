# What If One Nuclear Warhead Hit London? The Eight Minutes Before the Flash (Simulation)

Episode 021. Draft 2, 2026-11-28. Target about ten to eleven minutes at 138 words per minute. Each paragraph is one narration unit; the `[TAG]` on a section header is the on-screen corner tag. Source refs in brackets are for the fact screener and are not read aloud. The simulated strike has no calendar date and is not a forecast.

## Cold open [SIMULATION]

Eight forty-nine in the morning: one hundred kilotons detonate over Westminster. [C]

This is a simulation built from published numbers, and it has not happened. [C]

Nine minutes earlier, a Russian submarine in the Norwegian Sea launches two missiles. One flies to London. The other flies to a naval base in Scotland, and it lands first. [C]

It is the morning rush hour. The Scottish flash comes just over a minute before the London one. [C]

So why does the first warhead fall on Scotland? And what would three days of government advice mean after the second? [C]

## Chapter 01: the boats on the Clyde [REAL DATA]

On any given day, a Royal Navy submarine is somewhere at sea with nuclear missiles aboard. Since April nineteen sixty-nine, there has not been a single break. [A14, B4]

Patrols that once lasted two months now run a hundred and fifty to two hundred days. [A15]

Hold on to that unbroken patrol, and to the minute between the two flashes. Both start here. [C]

The boats are based at Faslane, on the Clyde in Scotland, about five hundred and ninety kilometres from central London. Four Vanguard-class submarines, each with sixteen missile tubes. One is normally in overhaul, so three are operational. [A11, A18]

About forty warheads sit on the one boat at sea at any time. Britain stopped publishing its numbers in twenty twenty-one, so the rest is outside estimates: about a hundred and twenty available, and about two hundred and twenty-five in all. [A7, A9, A10]

The spare warheads are stored at Coulport, about three kilometres from Faslane. [A18]

London is the other end of the line: about nine million people on sixteen hundred square kilometres. One base holds the deterrent. One city holds the government. [A23]

And in October, that government began telling households what to do when things stop working. [A2]

## Chapter 02: four minutes, fourteen days, three days [REAL HISTORY]

Britain once had a number for this. From nineteen fifty-three, the four-minute warning ran from an RAF headquarters through police posts to thousands of sirens. The name came from the estimated gap between confirming a missile attack and impact. [B3]

It ended in nineteen ninety-two, and the siren network was largely dismantled. In our simulation, the flights last seven point six and eight point eight minutes. [B3, C]

In May nineteen eighty, the booklet Protect and Survive went on public sale. It told households to build a fallout room and stay at home for fourteen days, in the inner refuge for the first forty-eight hours. [B5]

Now the guidance is shorter. On the twenty-second of October twenty twenty-six, the Cabinet Office launched Be Ready, a three-year campaign. It asks households to cope for about three days if power, water, phone and internet fail. [A2]

Be Ready is not a nuclear campaign. It names storms and wildfires and does not mention nuclear weapons, and reports quote the Defence Secretary saying there was no specific thing the government wants people to prepare for. [A3, A4]

So this episode asks something narrower: what would that kit and that list assume in the first hour of a nuclear strike? [A2, A3]

But first, how does a strike on London start? [C]

## Chapter 03: the trigger [SIMULATION]

In our simulation, a crisis between Russia and NATO has run for weeks, and Russia decides on a limited strike on the British capital and on the base of its deterrent. That decision is our invention. No government has said it. [C]

The weapon is an R-30 Bulava, fired from a Borei-class submarine in the Norwegian Sea. The launch point is simulated. A submarine-launched missile is chosen because the flight is short and leaves the least time to react. [C]

Published estimates of Bulava yields run from one hundred to one hundred and fifty kilotons. We choose one hundred, about seven times the Hiroshima bomb. [C, A25]

Britain has never published the yield of its own warhead, so this is not a claim about it. [A13]

Two warheads, one each. The first is aimed at Faslane and Coulport, about one thousand four hundred kilometres from the launch point. The second is aimed at Westminster and Whitehall, about one thousand eight hundred and forty kilometres away. [C]

Faslane goes first because the attack is meant to remove the deterrent. One boat is always at sea, so the boats in port are not the whole force. [A10, A14, C]

That answers the first question. What it leaves open is the eight minutes. [C]

## Chapter 04: minute by minute [SIMULATION]

Minute zero is eight-forty. The missiles leave the water in the Norwegian Sea. [C]

Nothing yet is visible from Britain. The sky over London is a normal morning sky. The missiles are in flight, and the flight takes less than nine minutes. [C]

The clocks matter more than the maps. Faslane is hit at about eight forty-eight. London is hit at about eight forty-nine. In between, a city of nine million goes on with its morning. [C, A23]

At seven point six minutes, the first warhead reaches Faslane and bursts on the ground. The fireball reaches about half a kilometre from the point of burst. Blast of twenty pounds per square inch reaches just over that. Five pounds per square inch reaches two and a half kilometres. [C]

A ground burst throws up earth, and the earth comes down as fallout. The engine marks Faslane as heavy local fallout. [C]

If the wind is from the west, the commonest direction in southern Britain, the plume drifts east over central Scotland. We choose that wind. We model no dose and no towns. [C]

The old four-minute warning ended in nineteen ninety-two. Today's radiation advice, go in, stay in, tune in, does not mention a nuclear weapon. [B3, A6]

At eight point eight minutes, the second warhead bursts in the air over Whitehall. [C]

One warhead. One city. The map now shows what that single burst does, ring by ring. [C]

## Chapter 05: one warhead, ring by ring [SIMULATION]

The fireball is three hundred and eighty metres in radius, about seven hundred and seventy metres across. Everything inside is vaporised. [C]

At six hundred and forty metres, the blast reaches twenty pounds per square inch. Heavy buildings do not stand. [C]

At three point one kilometres, it falls to five pounds per square inch. Most houses collapse. That circle covers thirty-one square kilometres, about two percent of Greater London. [C, A23]

At four point two kilometres, bare skin takes third-degree burns from the flash. [C]

At eight and a half kilometres, the blast is one pound per square inch. Windows shatter, and flying glass injures people far from the fireball. [C]

Because this is an airburst, local fallout is small. The smoke and dust of a burning city are not modelled here. [C]

On average about five thousand eight hundred people live on each square kilometre of Greater London, and the morning rush moves them around. We still do not count who is inside these rings. [A23]

The rings are the engine's numbers for a one hundred kiloton airburst, and a larger warhead would draw larger ones. [C, A25]

No published study supplies a figure for this warhead on this city, and we will not make one up. We do not model the effect on electronics. [D, C]

That is the first minute. The rest is the day after. [C]

## Chapter 06: the day after [SIMULATION]

On the map, the first change is a zone. The question was what three days of advice mean. Be Ready's list is water, long-life food, a torch, batteries, a power bank and a radio. Inside the five-pound ring, those things are in houses the engine says collapse. [A2, C]

The second change is a plume, far to the north. Back to the question: the advice for a radiation emergency is to stay in for one to two days. Protect and Survive asked for fourteen. Neither was written for a plume that begins at Britain's submarine base. [A6, B5, C]

The third is a ring of broken glass. Three days was the planning number for storms and outages: a few days without power, water and phone. Here the centre of government and a transport hub are inside the damage, and London produces about a fifth of the British economy. [A2, A24, C]

The fourth is a radio. The advice says tune in, and a battery radio is on the Be Ready list. The advice does not say what a nuclear detonation sounds like on air, because the radiation page names power plants and transport accidents as its examples. [A2, A6]

The fifth is a command line that must hold. Only the Prime Minister can authorise the use of British nuclear weapons. In this simulation, the centre of government is inside the five-pound ring. What happens to that authority is a question we pose and do not answer. [A17, C]

The sixth is a route that does not change. The submarine on patrol keeps patrolling. The stated doctrine is extreme circumstances of self-defence, with deliberate ambiguity. [A10, A14, A17]

What that boat is ordered to do is the one thing this simulation does not decide. It has not happened. [C]
