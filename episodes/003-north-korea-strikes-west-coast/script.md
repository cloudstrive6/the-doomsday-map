## Cold open [SIMULATION]

Minute zero. Three oh five in the morning on the Pacific coast of America. Seattle is asleep. In the mountains of Alaska, a duty officer sees several tracks appear on a screen. [C]

The tracks are ballistic missiles, and they have just left North Korea. From launch to impact, the clock reads about twenty-six minutes. [C]

What you are about to watch is a simulation. It has not happened. No crisis on the map is real, and no date is attached to it. But the missiles, the interceptors and the clock are built from published numbers, and the question is real: what could North Korea reach, and what could stop it? Every real figure on screen has a source, and every choice we made is labelled. [A9, C]

## Chapter 01: what North Korea has [REAL DATA]

The Stockholm International Peace Research Institute estimates that North Korea has assembled about sixty nuclear warheads. A year earlier, it said fifty. [A1]

Counts disagree. Some put the number near fifty. A South Korean institute view runs as high as one hundred and twenty. SIPRI says there is material for at least thirty more, about ninety in all. [A2, A3]

SIPRI says nearly all of those warheads are probably single-stage fission devices. Only a few may be thermonuclear. [A4]

North Korea has conducted six nuclear tests. The last was on the third of September, twenty seventeen. Estimates of its yield run from about fifty to two hundred and fifty kilotons. [A6, A7, B5]

To deliver a warhead across the Pacific, it needs an intercontinental missile. The Defense Intelligence Agency put the number at ten or fewer in twenty twenty-five. [A8]

The Hwasong-17 is liquid-fuelled and road-mobile, with a stated range of up to fifteen thousand kilometres. The Hwasong-18 burns solid fuel, so it does not need long fuelling at the pad. That means less warning. [A12, A13]

The United States intelligence community says North Korea's intercontinental missiles can already reach the American homeland. [A9]

But here is the caveat. SIPRI says there is no public evidence that North Korea has built an operational warhead for an intercontinental missile. And no test has ever flown a full-range path with a real warhead. [A5, A10, A14, A19]

## Chapter 02: how long would it take [REAL HISTORY]

In July twenty seventeen, North Korea fired its first intercontinental missile, a Hwasong-14. Twenty-four days later it fired a second. It flew for forty-seven minutes. Analysts said Los Angeles and Chicago were in range. [B2, B3]

Four months after that, a Hwasong-15 flew for fifty-three minutes. In October twenty twenty-four, a Hwasong-19 flew for eighty-six. It is the last long-range North Korean flight to date. [B6, B10]

Those numbers are not the warning time. Every one of those flights was lofted: fired at a steep, high angle, to land near North Korea. [A19, B6]

A real attack takes a flatter path. Estimates by the Union of Concerned Scientists put Los Angeles at thirty-eight minutes, and San Francisco at about thirty-three to thirty-four. Another analyst, John Schilling, put Seattle at under thirty. [A18]

So the honest answer is: roughly half an hour. [A18]

## Chapter 03: the shield [REAL DATA]

America's defence of its own territory is a system called Ground-based Midcourse Defense. It has forty-four interceptors. Forty are at Fort Greely, in Alaska. Four are at Vandenberg, in California. [A20]

An interceptor does not explode near a missile. It collides with the warhead in space, in the middle of its flight. [A22]

In scripted tests, the Arms Control Association counts twelve successful intercepts in twenty-one attempts. Roughly half to sixty percent. [A21]

In twenty nineteen, the system fired two interceptors at one target for the first time. Both worked. A real engagement would likely use more than one interceptor for each warhead, so forty-four interceptors do not mean forty-four missiles stopped. [A22, A23, B8]

Critics point out that those tests were scripted, and used no realistic decoys. [A24]

The next generation of interceptor is not fielded. Golden Dome, the plan for a bigger shield, has a Congressional Budget Office estimate of about one point two trillion dollars over twenty years. It is a plan, not a roof. [A25, A27]

## Chapter 04: the trigger [SIMULATION]

Our simulation begins with a decision. A crisis on the Korean peninsula has gone wrong. North Korea's law says the final order to use nuclear weapons belongs to one man, Kim Jong Un. In our story, that order is given. [A28, C]

We do not name a cause. We do not say it is likely. The order is for a limited strike: six missiles. In our story, two are intercepted and four get through, one warhead on each of four targets. That is a choice we made, not a forecast. Six sits inside the Defense Intelligence Agency's count of ten or fewer. [A8, S6, C]

Of the four that get through, two are Hwasong-18 class and two are Hwasong-17. We do not use the Hwasong-20, because it has never been flown. [A15, C]

The targets are our choice. Seattle, the nearest big city. The submarine base at Bangor. San Francisco. And the missile defence site at Vandenberg. [C]

The yields are also our assumption: two hundred and fifty kilotons on Seattle, the top of the range for the twenty seventeen test, and one hundred kilotons on each of the others. A smaller fission weapon of a few tens of kilotons would make every ring that follows much smaller. [A5, A7, C]

## Chapter 05: minute by minute [SIMULATION]

Minute zero. We assume satellites in orbit see the heat of the launches within a minute or two, and that the first alert reaches the missile defence command almost at once. [C]

Minute one. A second Hwasong-18 leaves its launcher. The first is already above the atmosphere. [C]

Minute three. A Hwasong-17 lifts off from near Pyongyang. Minute four, the fourth. [C]

In our story, two more missiles are launched as well. Interceptors destroy both. That is a choice we made, not a forecast of how the real system would perform. [A21, A24, S6, C]

Minutes later, the warheads separate from their rockets. They are small and fast, falling through space toward the coast. [C]

In the middle of the flight, the interceptor window opens. The defence has minutes, not hours, to find the real warheads, and to shoot more than once at each. In our story, it does not stop these four. [A22, A23, C]

Somewhere along the coast, phones are about to buzz. In January twenty eighteen, a false missile alert in Hawaii reportedly stayed uncorrected for thirty-eight minutes. A true one would leave less time than that. [B7, C]

Minute twenty-five and a half. The first warhead reaches Seattle. [C]

## Chapter 06: one warhead, ring by ring [SIMULATION]

It detonates in the air above downtown Seattle. Two hundred and fifty kilotons. [C]

The fireball is five hundred and fifty metres in radius. Everything inside it is vapour. [C]

Out to eight hundred and seventy metres, the blast adds twenty pounds per square inch of pressure. Even strong buildings are destroyed. [C]

Out to four point three kilometres, five pounds per square inch. Most houses collapse. That ring covers about fifty-seven square kilometres. [C]

Out to six kilometres, the flash itself causes third-degree burns on bare skin. [C]

And out to eleven and a half kilometres, one pound per square inch. Windows shatter, and the glass becomes a weapon. [C]

An airburst throws up little local fallout. The dust rises high, and where it falls depends on the weather. [C]

One minute later, a ground burst at Bangor, with heavy local fallout. Four minutes after that, an airburst over San Francisco. Then Vandenberg. The last warhead lands thirty-two minutes after the first launch. [C]

Four warheads. Five hundred and fifty kilotons in all. [C]

We give no casualty figure, because no published study supports one. [C]

## Chapter 07: the bill [REAL DATA]

A published study from Nagasaki University and the Nautilus Institute, in twenty twenty-three, modelled a Korean nuclear war. Its largest Korea scenario, with eighteen weapons and China joining in, comes to about two million one hundred thousand deaths within months. It covers the Korean region, not this strike. [A30]

The twenty twenty-three Strategic Posture Commission found that North Korea was on pace to deploy enough intercontinental missiles between twenty twenty-seven and twenty thirty-five to potentially challenge America's ground-based defences. [A29]

Real launches continue. The Yonhap news agency counted seventeen rounds of North Korean launches in twenty twenty-six by the end of October, all shorter range. After the twelfth of October launch, US Indo-Pacific Command said there was no immediate threat. [A31]

Congress has told the Pentagon to build a third interceptor site on the East Coast by twenty thirty-one. [A26]

## Chapter 08: when it stayed unused [REAL HISTORY]

In October two thousand six, North Korea tested its first nuclear weapon. Most analysts judged the yield below one kiloton. [B1]

In August twenty seventeen, an American president promised fire and fury. Days later, North Korea announced a plan to fire four missiles toward Guam. One account says it was later put on hold. [B4]

Those were a test and a threat. Our simulation asks what an actual attack would look like. [B4, C]

## Chapter 09: the clock [SIMULATION]

About twenty-six minutes to Seattle. About thirty-two to the last target. [C]

That is the window for a president to be woken, briefed and asked what to do. Our simulation stops at the last impact, because no one can say what comes next. [C]

## The decision [REAL DATA]

What is real is the arithmetic. About sixty warheads. Forty-four interceptors. And a flight of roughly half an hour. [A1, A20, A18]
