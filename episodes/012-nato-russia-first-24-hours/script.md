# NATO and Russia: The First 24 Hours of a Conventional War, and the Line It Stops At

Episode 012. A simulation of the first twenty-four hours of a conventional war between Russia and NATO, built from published force data, arms-control estimates and missile ranges. It has not happened. Each paragraph is one narration unit; bracketed refs are for the fact screener and are not read aloud.

## Cold open [SIMULATION]

Hour zero, northern Poland: a Russian missile lands on a NATO air base. [C]

This is a simulation, and it has not happened. [A1]

Three minutes earlier, one Iskander leaves a launcher in Kaliningrad. It has one hundred and eighty-four kilometres to fly, and the flight takes about three minutes. A flight that short leaves almost no warning. [A10, C]

The clock on the map reads twenty-four hours. How does a day of conventional war end, and what line does it stop at? [C]

## Chapter 01: a note to NATO [REAL DATA]

On the thirtieth of September, twenty twenty-six, Reuters reported a diplomatic note sent to NATO states. Russia said it had information that NATO was preparing an air and naval blockade of Kaliningrad. If NATO tried to cut it off, the note said, Russia was ready to use its entire arsenal, including nuclear weapons. [A2]

NATO's secretary-general answered with four words: stop the nuclear threats. NATO says it is a defensive alliance and has no such plan. [A2]

Two weeks later NATO ran its annual nuclear exercise, Steadfast Noon, in Italy, with no live weapons. NATO called it routine. [A3]

That note is the spark of our simulation. In our simulation, Moscow acts on its own warning, and the clock starts. [A2, C]

Kaliningrad is Russian land on the Baltic coast, about one million people, wedged between Poland and Lithuania. Russia has based Iskander missiles there since twenty eighteen. [A26]

Sources put the Iskander's range at between three hundred and fifty and five hundred kilometres. Either way, from Kaliningrad, Warsaw is in reach, and so are Vilnius and Riga. [A10, A9]

Between Kaliningrad and Belarus lies the Suwalki Gap, a strip of land about sixty-five kilometres wide. It is the shortest land link from Poland to the Baltic states. [A8]

So what has NATO actually promised to do if the first missile lands? [A6]

## Chapter 02: how close it has come [REAL HISTORY]

In ten days of September twenty twenty-five, about twenty Russian drones entered Poland. Three Russian fighter jets flew into Estonian airspace. Poland and Estonia both asked NATO for consultations under Article four. Our simulation begins where those incidents stop. [B7, A6]

Article four is talk. Article five is the promise: an armed attack on one is an attack on all. But each ally takes "such action as it deems necessary". It has been invoked once, after the attacks of September eleventh, two thousand and one. [A6]

In June nineteen ninety-nine, about two hundred Russian soldiers drove into Pristina airport in Kosovo ahead of NATO. NATO's commander wanted the runway blocked. A British general reportedly refused, saying he would not start the third world war for you. The account is disputed. [B3]

In November nineteen eighty-three, NATO rehearsed a shift from conventional to nuclear war in an exercise called Able Archer. A later US review said relations may have been put "on a hair trigger", and that the Soviets were genuinely worried. Our simulation ends at that same step. [B1]

How fast would it unfold? An Iskander from Kaliningrad needs about three minutes to reach a Polish base. An Oreshnik from Belarus, in our engine, needs six. [C, A13]

Which base does Russia hit first, and why that one? [C]

## Chapter 03: the trigger [SIMULATION]

In our simulation, the first hours are Russian, and they are a choice. Jamming and cyber attacks come first, the pattern seen in the real incidents. Then come the missiles. These are not forecasts. [C, A5, B7]

At hour zero, one Iskander hits Malbork Air Base in Poland, a Polish air base within easy reach of Kaliningrad. The aim, in our simulation, is to hit it before NATO can use it against the oblast. [C]

Half an hour in, a second Iskander, launched from the Pskov region, hits Adazi Base in Latvia, where a Canadian-led NATO battlegroup is stationed. It travels two hundred and fifty kilometres in three and a half minutes. [A7, C]

At hour one, a Kalibr cruise missile fired from a ship in the Baltic heads for Lielvarde Air Base, also in Latvia. It flies for about twenty-one minutes, so it gives far more warning than the Iskanders. [C]

All three launch points are simulated, and every base is a real, public location. The warheads are conventional. [C]

NATO invokes Article four at once. In our simulation, by about hour six, it invokes Article five. Will NATO hit back at Kaliningrad, and with what? [A6, C]

## Chapter 04: hour by hour [SIMULATION]

Hour four. An Oreshnik, a ballistic missile that carries several warheads, flies from eastern Belarus to Rzeszow-Jasionka airport in Poland. It travels seven hundred and seventy-four kilometres in about six minutes. [C, A13]

We make it conventional. CSIS does not confirm a nuclear Oreshnik, and Russia's two real uses, on Dnipro and on Lviv, carried no confirmed nuclear warhead. Rzeszow is the hub for aid to Ukraine. [A13, C]

Hour five. NATO answers, and in this simulation the United States joins. Western officers have said that key Kaliningrad installations would likely be the first target, according to one report. A cruise missile from aircraft over northern Poland hits Chkalovsk air base. [A3, C]

Hour six. Another hits Baltiysk, the Baltic Fleet's main base. At hour six and a half, a third hits the dense belt of air defence that covers the oblast. Where exactly it stands is not in open reporting, so the point is simulated. [A26, A12, C]

Hour thirteen. An Iskander from Kaliningrad hits Bemowo Piskie in Poland, where a US battalion group is stationed, by one count about eight hundred soldiers. It is one hundred and six kilometres from launch to target, three minutes. [A8, C]

Then, from hour fourteen to hour twenty-one, the squeeze. In our simulation, Kaliningrad's air cover is thin, and its bases are burning. Russian doctrine lists attacks that threaten its territory among the conditions for nuclear use. The war stays conventional and bounded. No ground push is modelled. [A19, C]

Hour twenty-two. One Iskander-M, with a nuclear warhead, leaves Kaliningrad for the open Baltic Sea. No ship and no coast is the target. It is a warning shot, and it is our choice. One reading of Russian signalling holds that such a shot is possible. We do not claim it is Russian policy. [A10, A17, C]

The Iskander is dual-capable: it can carry either kind of warhead. The flight is two hundred and thirty-four kilometres, about three and a half minutes. [A10, C]

No open source gives its yield. So in this simulation, we use ten kilotons, about half of Nagasaki. What does ten kilotons do, over water? [C]

## Chapter 05: one warhead, ring by ring [SIMULATION]

The warhead bursts in the air above the sea, well over a hundred kilometres from any coast. Every number in this chapter comes from our engine, and every one is an estimate. [C]

The fireball is about one hundred and fifty metres in radius, so about three hundred metres across. [C]

At just under three hundred metres, the pressure is twenty pounds per square inch. On land, reinforced concrete would fail. [C]

At one point four six kilometres, five pounds per square inch. On land, most buildings would be flattened. This ring covers about six point seven square kilometres. [C]

Out to one point six kilometres, the flash is enough for third-degree burns on bare skin. [C]

At three point nine five kilometres, one pound per square inch. On land, windows would shatter. [C]

Out here, there is nothing in those rings. The engine finds little local fallout. We give no casualty figure, because none is published for this scenario. [C]

It is one warhead, and it would still be the first nuclear weapon used in war since Nagasaki. What the world does with it is the rest of the simulation. [C, A1]

## Chapter 06: the day after [SIMULATION]

The day ended at a line. At hour twenty-four, seven places on the map are marked struck: Malbork, Adazi, Lielvarde, Rzeszow, Chkalovsk, Baltiysk and Bemowo Piskie, and a belt of Kaliningrad's air defence is partly down. The front has not moved. [C]

Rzeszow goes dark. It is the Polish hub for aid to Ukraine, and in our simulation its runway is closed, so every route that passed through it has to find another. [A9, C]

Over the Baltic, the sky is empty. Kyiv said Russian jamming pushed drones off course in the alerts of November twenty twenty-six. In our simulation, civil flights avoid the region. Under the water, the cables are exposed: Estlink Two, between Finland and Estonia, was cut in December twenty twenty-four. [A5, A27, C]

No plume is drawn. An air burst over water means little local fallout, and we assume no wind. That is not the same as safe. [C]

In Brussels, an Article five table. Each ally decides its own action. Three options sit on it as categories: a conventional reply, a pause and talks, or a reply in kind. We do not pick one. [A6, C]

The last treaty limits on strategic forces ended when New START lapsed in February twenty twenty-six, and Russia's nonstrategic weapons, roughly fifteen hundred to two thousand warheads, were never covered by any treaty. [A21, A18]

The front has not moved. A line has been crossed. [C]
