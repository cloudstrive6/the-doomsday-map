Minute sixteen, Washington State: nuclear warheads reach American soil. [C]

This is a simulation built from published numbers, and it has not happened. [C]

Eleven minutes earlier, Russian missiles left their submarines off both American coasts. Two minutes before the flash, American missiles left their silos. By minute fifty-three, it is over. [C]

## Chapter 01: the real forces [REAL DATA]

On the twentieth of May, twenty twenty-six, the United States Air Force fired an unarmed Minuteman missile from Vandenberg, California. It said the test had been scheduled for years. [A21]

The day before, Russia had begun a three-day nuclear exercise: sixty-four thousand troops, thirteen submarines, and the stated aim of practising the use of nuclear forces under the threat of aggression. [A23]

These are the missiles from the opening. In our simulation, the ones from submarines cross an ocean in about ten to fourteen minutes. The ones from silos take about thirty. [A12, C]

In February twenty twenty-six, the last limit on the two arsenals expired. For the first time in decades, no treaty caps either side. [A1, B8]

Russia keeps about eighteen hundred strategic warheads deployed, the United States about seventeen hundred. America's land force is four hundred Minuteman missiles, one warhead each. Russia has about three hundred and twenty-four land missiles and thirteen ballistic-missile submarines. [A5, A6, A7, A9]

Those are the forces. The harder question is time: how long does anyone get to decide? [A12]

## Chapter 02: ten minutes [REAL HISTORY]

The United States keeps almost all of its four hundred land missiles ready to fire within minutes. Launch on warning means firing after the alarm, before the first enemy warhead lands. Bruce Blair, a former launch officer, estimates the president would have at most about ten minutes to decide. [A13, A14]

On the twenty-sixth of September, nineteen eighty-three, a Soviet satellite system reported five American missiles heading for Russia. The duty officer, Stanislav Petrov, judged it a false alarm: a real first strike would involve hundreds of missiles. The cause was sunlight on cloud tops. That night the warning was wrong, and a person said so. Our simulation asks what happens when the warning is right. [B2, C]

On the twenty-fifth of January, nineteen ninety-five, a Norwegian and American science rocket was launched from northern Norway. The notice of the launch never reached Russian radar crews, who read the track as a possible Trident missile. The nuclear briefcase was brought to President Yeltsin. Russian forces stayed on alert for about eight minutes, out of a window of about ten. No order was given. [B4]

Ten minutes is also about how long a submarine missile takes to arrive. So what does a war look like when the warning is real? [A12, B4]

## Chapter 03: the trigger [SIMULATION]

In our simulation, a crisis in Europe has already turned nuclear. We leave its cause unnamed. This is one chosen path, not a forecast, and it shows a selection of the first hour, not the whole war: three hundred and seventy-five warheads, a fraction of what the two sides deploy. [C]

Minute zero. A single low-yield missile leaves Kaliningrad oblast, launch point simulated, aimed at Ramstein Air Base in Germany. Princeton's published Plan A also opens with tactical use in Europe. [C, A27]

It lands at minute eight point seven. The blast reaches about one and a half kilometres at five pounds per square inch, the engine's figure for a ten-kiloton airburst. [C]

It is the smallest strike of the hour. The rest are about nine times larger or more. [C]

At minute five, Russia has already launched its main wave at American forces: radars, submarine bases, command centres, silo fields. The submarine warheads, with the shortest flights, land first. [C]

Missiles leave land and sea at once, and the radars on the coasts are among the first things aimed at. In the simulation, the decision in Washington falls in the next nine minutes. What does it do? [C]

## Chapter 04: the first hour, minute by minute [SIMULATION]

Minute fourteen. Nine minutes after Russia's main wave leaves, the United States fires its Minuteman force on warning, inside Blair's ten minutes. This is a simulation choice, not a prediction of what any president would do. The Russian land missiles are nine minutes into a flight that will last another sixteen to twenty-two minutes. [C, A13, A14]

Minutes fifteen and sixteen. Warheads of about a hundred kilotons each strike the early-warning radar at Cape Cod, then the submarine base at Bangor, Washington, then the radar at Beale. [C]

Minute sixteen. American submarines fire at Russian bases and command centres. Their warheads are roughly a hundred kilotons each, and they fly nine to twelve minutes. [C]

Minute seventeen. Six warheads go to Washington and the Pentagon. The base at Kings Bay, Georgia, home to six Ohio-class submarines, follows at minute nineteen. [A9, C]

Minutes twenty-five to twenty-eight. The Northern Fleet base near Murmansk, the Pacific Fleet base at Vilyuchinsk, Plesetsk, Moscow's command centres. [C]

Minutes twenty-nine to thirty-six. The Russian land missiles arrive after about thirty minutes in flight. Minuteman is the force a first strike tries to catch before it leaves, which is why the silo fields come early. The three silo-field strikes carry sixty warheads each, of roughly a hundred to three hundred kilotons. They hit Minot, Malmstrom and Warren, Cheyenne Mountain, Offutt and the bomber bases. The strike on Offutt uses larger warheads, up to about seven hundred and fifty kilotons. By now the American land missiles are already in the air. The warheads land on silos that are mostly empty, and they still land. [C, A12]

Minutes thirty-nine to forty-three. The American missiles arrive at Russian missile divisions: Kozelsk, Teykovo, Tatishchevo, Novosibirsk, Uzhur, Dombarovsky. [C]

Minute forty. The last submarines fire at cities, the final phase in Plan A as well. New York, Los Angeles, Saint Petersburg and Moscow take eight warheads each, a count we chose. The last impact is at minute fifty-three. [C, A27]

In all, the engine counts twenty-seven strikes and three hundred and seventy-five warheads, about seventy-nine megatons, in fifty-three minutes. That is the whole of our hour. [C]

So what does one of those warheads do on the ground? [C]

## Chapter 05: one warhead, ring by ring [SIMULATION]

This is the strike from minute fifteen point eight. Take Naval Base Kitsap, Bangor, on Puget Sound. Eight Ohio-class submarines are based here. Our simulation puts four warheads on it. We look at one: a hundred kilotons, burst on the ground. [A9, C]

The fireball reaches about four hundred and eighty metres from the centre. Inside five hundred and ten metres, the pressure is twenty pounds per square inch. [C]

At two and a half kilometres, five pounds per square inch. Heat that causes third-degree burns reaches four point two kilometres. [C]

At six point eight kilometres, one pound per square inch. These rings describe blast and heat on land. We give no death toll for them. [C]

Inside five pounds per square inch lies about twenty square kilometres. Four warheads would overlap, so we show one. Four on one base is our choice: a counterforce strike aims to make sure of its target. [C]

Because it is a ground burst, the fallout is heavy and local. In our simulation the wind blows from the west, so the plume runs east, across Puget Sound. Seattle, about thirty kilometres away, is not a target here. [C]

By one count, about nine hundred and fifty American warheads are at sea on submarines, and Bangor is one of the two bases that host the boats. Taking out the base is the logic of hitting the boats before they leave. [A9, C]

## Chapter 06: the day after [SIMULATION]

How does one missile become a war in under an hour? In the simulation it does so by minute fifty-three. If the war did not stop here, the map keeps changing. [C]

First, fallout. The silo fields in the American plains are hit by ground bursts, which leave heavy local fallout. In our simulation the wind blows from the west, so the plume drifts east. The engine names the category, heavy local fallout, but gives no distances, so neither do we. [C]

Second, the cities. Princeton's Plan A runs in the same order as our hour: tactical use, strikes on nuclear forces, then population centres. Its estimate, for a larger war, is more than ninety million people dead and injured in the first few hours, direct effects only. That is the study's number, not ours. Our selection is smaller. [A27]

Third, the sky. One climate model of a far larger war, put at a hundred and fifty million tonnes of soot, finds global temperatures falling sharply for years. It is one model, not a forecast. Our three hundred and seventy-five warheads are nowhere near that war. [A29, C]

Fourth, the harvest. In a Nature Food study of twenty twenty-two, a war of about forty-four hundred weapons cuts world crop calories by about ninety percent within four years. More than five billion people would die of famine, in that one model, with no adaptation assumed. [A28]

In our simulation the clock stops at minute fifty-three. We do not know what comes next, and neither does anyone else. [C, A27, A28]
