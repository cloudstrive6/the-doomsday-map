## Chapter 01: the island that is a runway [REAL DATA]

During the exercise Valiant Shield in twenty twenty-six, photos showed an Army Dark Eagle hypersonic missile on Guam. The Pacific command confirmed the photos were taken there, and did not say whether the weapon stayed. [A24]

Guam is an American island of about one hundred and seventy thousand people, and the Defense Department owns about twenty-nine percent of it. [A16]

It is also the island in our cold open, the one with the flash at one forty-three. [C]

Andersen Air Force Base has two parallel runways, of about three point two and three point four kilometres. Bombers rotate through it, and since twenty twenty they deploy ad hoc rather than permanently. Naval Base Guam, about forty-eight kilometres away, berths submarines. [A14, A17]

About two thousand three hundred kilometres to the north-west, Kadena Air Base on Okinawa has two runways of about three point seven kilometres, and an Army Patriot battalion to defend them. [A13, A15]

What reaches them is the DF-26. CSIS calls it China's first conventionally armed ballistic missile able to reach Guam. Western media call it the Guam killer, a nickname, not an official name. [A5, A8]

It has a range of about four thousand kilometres. One warhead can be swapped for another, nuclear or conventional, and most are conventional. Estimates put China at roughly two hundred and fifty to three hundred launchers. [A5, A6]

Guam's new missile defence system is not due until late twenty twenty-seven. The first interception test from the island was in December twenty twenty-four. [A23, B10]

So has anyone ever come close to firing at Guam? [B5]

## Chapter 02: the base-attack record [REAL HISTORY]

Just before eight in the morning on the seventh of December nineteen forty-one, Japanese carrier planes hit Pearl Harbor. About two hundred American aircraft were destroyed, by the National Park Service's count. Every Pacific Fleet carrier was away at sea. An air base cannot move. A carrier can. [B1]

On the ninth of August twenty seventeen, North Korea said it was studying a plan to fire four missiles over Japan into waters near Guam. It never launched. A threat alone made Guam a target in the news. [B5]

On the twenty-sixth of August twenty twenty, China fired a DF-26B and a DF-21D into the South China Sea, the first public test of its so-called carrier-killer missiles into water. The DF-26 in our simulation is the same family of weapon. [B7]

Every one of those was slow: a fleet crossing an ocean, a threat made in words, a test in empty water. [B1, B5, B7]

What if the missile were not a test, and the flight took thirteen minutes? [C]

## Chapter 03: the order [SIMULATION]

In our simulation, the diplomacy has already failed, and we do not say why. The clock starts at the first ballistic launch. We call it minute zero. [C]

Twenty-five minutes before that, an H-6K bomber over the East China Sea releases one CJ-20 cruise missile toward Kadena. It is subsonic. It is timed to arrive last. [A9, C]

An H-6K can carry up to six of those missiles, but bombers face a high risk of being detected. We use one. [A9, C]

One report says a Chinese air force video in twenty twenty showed an H-6 bomber striking an airfield laid out like Andersen. [A29]

At minute zero, two DF-26 missiles leave for Andersen Air Force Base. One carries a conventional warhead. The other carries a nuclear one. [C]

Why so many missiles at one base? Accuracy. Analysts estimate the DF-26's circular error probable at one hundred and fifty to four hundred and fifty metres, so a planner needs many missiles to be confident of damaging a target. [A7]

RAND wrote in July twenty twenty-five that China's stockpile of intermediate-range missiles grew from about twenty in twenty twelve to about five hundred in twenty twenty-three, and that air base defence "has not kept pace". [A20]

That second missile is our choice, not a forecast. China's declared policy is no first use. We give it ten kilotons, the top of the Pentagon's below-ten-kiloton estimate for low-yield warheads, and about half the bomb dropped on Nagasaki. The Pentagon says China is probably pursuing such warheads. [A10, A11, C]

In Pentagon-sponsored war games, the side playing China typically opens by attacking Guam's bases. In the CNAS game Dangerous Straits, in twenty twenty-two, China struck American bases in Japan and Guam in the opening. We added one nuclear shot to test the threshold. [A25, C]

The risk is that a defender cannot tell the two missiles apart. Analysts warn that a conventional strike by a nuclear-capable force can look nuclear. [A27]

Seven weapons, one nuclear. The first one to land does not land on Guam. [C]

## Chapter 04: the first nineteen minutes [SIMULATION]

Minute one. A third DF-26 leaves, aimed at Naval Base Guam. [C]

Minute two. Two more DF-26s leave: one for Kadena, one for the US Navy's base at Yokosuka, Japan. [C]

Minute three. A DF-17 hypersonic glider leaves Fujian for Kadena, conventional. [C]

Minute eight. The first impact. A DF-26 reaches Kadena after six minutes of flight. We do not count which Patriot interceptors work. The launcher we placed for it is only about eight hundred and sixty-six kilometres away. [C]

A little after minute ten, the DF-17 lands at Kadena, after seven minutes of flight. A little before minute eleven, a DF-26 lands at Yokosuka, after nine, at the home port of the only US carrier based in Japan. We assume it is already at sea. [A18, C]

Guam is farther. The flights to Guam run from nearly thirteen minutes to fourteen and a half. [C]

Minute thirteen. One forty-three in the morning on Guam. A DF-26 detonates over Andersen Air Force Base. In this simulation, nothing stops it: one missile against a saturating wave. [C]

The flash is the cold open. Seconds later, at about fourteen, the missile for Naval Base Guam lands. At fourteen and a half, the conventional DF-26 lands on Andersen. [C]

At about nineteen, the last weapon, the cruise missile, reaches Kadena. [C]

In order of arrival: a DF-26 at Kadena, the glider at Kadena, a DF-26 at Yokosuka, the nuclear DF-26 at Andersen, the DF-26 at Naval Base Guam, the conventional DF-26 at Andersen, and last the cruise missile at Kadena. Seven weapons, four bases, eleven minutes from the first impact to the last. [C]

So what did that one warhead reach? [C]

## Chapter 05: one warhead, ring by ring [SIMULATION]

The burst is in the air, above the flight line, at one forty-three in the morning. The fireball is about three hundred metres across. [C]

Out to three hundred metres, the overpressure is twenty pounds per square inch. That is the inner ring, and it sits on the base. [C]

At one and a half kilometres, it is five pounds per square inch. That ring covers about six point seven square kilometres. Most residential buildings in it collapse. It reaches along a large part of one runway. [A14, C]

Remember the accuracy estimate. A miss of four hundred and fifty metres, the top of that range, would still land inside this ring. [A7, C]

Ten kilotons is the whole nuclear total for this hour. The other six weapons carry conventional warheads, so the engine gives them no blast rings. [C]

Just past the five-psi ring, at one point six kilometres, bare skin takes third-degree burns. [C]

At nearly four kilometres, it is one pound per square inch. Windows break. That circle covers about forty-nine square kilometres, roughly nine percent of an island of about five hundred and forty. [A16, C]

Naval Base Guam is outside every ring. So is almost all of the island. [A14, C]

We give no death toll. No published study supplies one for this strike, and we will not invent it. [C]

The rings stop at four kilometres. What the burst does to the Pacific does not. [C]

## Chapter 06: the day after [SIMULATION]

The question from the first minute was how many fighters can still take off. In our simulation, the published studies give a range, and none of them covers a nuclear burst on a flight line. [A19, C]

On the map, the runway under the rings goes dark. A Stimson Center study of conventional cratering puts the loss at nearly two days of fighter operations from Guam. For bases in Japan, about twelve days. These are one estimate with assumed inputs, not outcomes. [A19, C]

An older RAND estimate assumes fifty or a hundred missiles at one base. Our hour uses seven weapons in total. [A21, C]

Over Japan, the tanker routes are the ones to watch. By the same study, tankers there could be unable to operate for over a month. Japan has about thirty-six military airfields and only about a hundred and forty aircraft shelters, by one estimate. One account says the Air Force built its crater repair around twenty-four to thirty-six hours. [A18, A19, A22]

There is no plume on this map. An airburst of this size leaves little local fallout, so we draw none. [C]

The decision moves to Washington. Its declared policy is to use nuclear weapons only in extreme circumstances to defend its vital interests and its allies. Its options include a low-yield Trident warhead of about five kilotons, a conventional answer, or talks. We stop the clock before it chooses. [A28, C]

The bill is not on the map. Bloomberg Economics estimates that a war over Taiwan could cost the world about ten trillion dollars in its first year. [A31]

The missiles on this map exist. The war on it has not happened. [A1]
