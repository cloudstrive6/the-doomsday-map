## C. Simulation assumptions (SIMULATION tag, stated as assumptions in the script)

Decided 2026-11-10 by the scenario designer. Numbers come from `scenario.computed.json` (studio/scenario.py). Everything below is a simulation choice, not a prediction, and none of it is a fact. Weak research rows are not relied on as fact: A16 (no published Russian tactical yield), A25 (single-source territory share), A15 (single-source ranges), A19 (Belarus deployment unconfirmed) and A30 (inference about indistinguishable flight) are used only as labelled assumptions or not at all.

**Clock.** Day numbers only. No calendar date, battlefield detail or real unit appears inside the simulation. The engine clock is set to a winter morning (07:20 UTC, 09:20 in Kyiv, low sun) only for lighting; the script never says that date. t0 is the launch, "minute zero".

**Escalation logic and why.**
- Trigger (assumption, label it): before Day 1 a Ukrainian counter-offensive threatens a Russian-held sector, and Moscow frames the loss as the kind of "critical threat to territorial integrity" its 2024 doctrine describes (A17). The doctrine names no target list and does not say what Russia would do; the script must say so. No named unit, commander or leader decision is shown.
- Why one weapon: the point of the scenario is the first-use decision, not an exchange. One low-yield weapon on a military target is the "escalate to de-escalate" case that commentators debate; the script may call it an idea analysts describe, not Russian policy (cite the A and B rows when written).
- Why this target: a military assembly area in open farmland, away from cities and away from the Zaporizhzhia plant (A27), so the scene needs no casualty claim and no fallout over a town. It is a staging area for Ukrainian reserves, invented, with no real name. The site is labelled "(location simulated)".
- Warning time: the weapon is an Iskander-M, the dual-capable missile Russia fires conventionally at Ukraine every week (A14). Whether defenders could tell a nuclear warhead from a conventional one in flight is an inference (A30); say "it would look like any other Iskander". Engine: 130 km, flight about 3 minutes, so no meaningful warning.

**Strike group (one strike).** Day 1, 09:20 Kyiv: one Iskander-M from a simulated launch site in Belgorod region (50.55 N, 36.60 E) at a simulated staging area in eastern Kharkiv region (49.45 N, 37.20 E). Weapon class srbm, yield 15 kt, airburst, one warhead. Yield rationale: no open source gives Russian tactical yields (A16), so 15 kt is chosen to equal Hiroshima (A22, B1); the script must say "we assume". Lukashenko's "three times Hiroshima" is unverified and is not used.

**Hero strike: the staging area, ring by ring (engine numbers, 15 kt air burst).**
- Fireball: radius 0.18 km (about 360 m across).
- 20 psi: 0.34 km.
- 5 psi: 1.67 km radius, about 8.8 km2 (most ordinary buildings badly damaged or collapsed; the script describes it in land terms, no persons).
- Third-degree burns: 1.91 km (farther than the 5 psi ring in clear air).
- 1 psi: 4.52 km (windows shatter, glass injuries).
- Fallout: little local fallout for an air burst (engine). Because the fireball does not touch the ground, the cloud is small compared with a ground burst. The wind is a simulation choice: from the west at Day 1, so any light fallout and the stem of the cloud drift east, toward the Russian-held side and the border, away from Kharkiv city (about 90 km north-west of ground zero, my map estimate, no engine number). Say "the wind we chose". Do not give a dose, a downwind distance or a contour: the engine supplies none and no published model was run for this site.
- Close-up: no `video/public/city/*.json` exists for open farmland. The director can fetch a map with `node scripts/fetch-city.mjs` at 49.45 N, 37.2 E (about 12 km wide) so the rings sit over fields, a rail line and a village-scale road net, or use a region shot at about 15 km width. The rings must be drawn only from the engine numbers above.

**Day after (day numbers; categories are real, the order and outcomes are the simulation's).**
- First 24 hours: the flash, the first reports (Ukraine's air force and Russia's defence ministry would each say something different), the US and NATO emergency meetings, the Burns-Naryshkin style channel of 2022 reopened (B7, B8 as precedent). The G7 and the UN Security Council meet. Radiation monitors in neighbouring countries and the IAEA report on what they see, and the readings are at background outside the immediate area; this last line is a modelling expectation for an air burst, not a measurement (use "would", not "did").
- Day 2 to 3: no ally answers with a nuclear weapon, which follows the stated line of "decisive consequences" (A21 Sullivan 2022) and treats the Petraeus 2022 view (B5, one retired general, not US policy) as one option: a conventional response against Russian forces. Sanctions and diplomatic isolation are announced. China and India distance themselves, which is a simulation choice and must be said as one. Russia says it will not use another weapon "for now". The script must not claim anyone's real intent.
- Day 4 to 7: oil, grain and shipping insurance markets react; flights are rerouted from the Ukrainian border area; Poland and the Baltic states raise alert levels; fear of a second use spreads, and the war continues on the front. What stops working in the first week is limited to what the strike and the response plausibly touch: civilian air corridors, border crossings, bank settlement links for Russia, and rail through the target district. The strike causes no grid collapse and no EMP effect (a 15 kt air burst this low has no wide-area EMP).
- Day 7 close: the taboo is broken, there is no ceasefire, and no one knows whether there will be a second weapon. The episode ends here, on the simulation's aftermath, with the open question of whether a norm that held since 1945 (B1) can be restored. No history chapter follows.

**What the script must not claim.**
- No death toll, injured count or "thousands" for the staging area. No published study covers this strike; Plan A (A23) models a full Russia-NATO war and must not be quoted for it.
- No real Russian yield, no real doctrine "target list", no real nuclear decision date, no real unit or person. The 2024 doctrine does not say Russia would use a weapon in this situation.
- No Western leader or Russian leader as a character; no claim that NATO or the US would respond in a particular way beyond "as stated" quotes (A21, B5) with their limits.
- No statement that such a strike is likely or planned; "it has not happened" is spoken once in the cold open.
- No fallout map, no dose and no contour; the engine gives none.
- Belarus-based weapons only as "Russia says" (A19). Do not use A25's 20 percent as a fact; "about a fifth, by one count".

## D. Figures the script must NOT use

- "Russia has 6,000 nuclear warheads" or "5,889" (older SIPRI/FAS totals): the March 2026 FAS figure is about 4,400 assigned and about 5,420 with retired warheads (A10).
- "Russia has 2,000 tactical nukes" as a fact: it is an upper bound ("up to 2,000", STRATCOM); FAS says about 1,794 with low confidence; the US government says 1,000 to 2,000 (A12).
- A Russian tactical yield stated as fact ("Russian tactical nukes are 1 to 100 kt" or "three times Hiroshima"): no open source supplies it; Lukashenko's "three times" is an unverified claim (A16).
- "Russian nuclear weapons are in Belarus" as confirmed fact: Putin and Lukashenko say so; FAS finds no conclusive public evidence as of March 2026 (A19). Say "Russia says".
- "Oreshnik reaches Brussels in 17 minutes" or "5,000 km range": Russian and Belarusian claims that Western experts doubt; outside estimates are about 1,000 to 1,600 km from observed use (A6).
- "Oreshnik carries nuclear warheads in Ukraine strikes": it is nuclear-capable, but the 2024 strike used dummy warheads and the 2026 payload is unclear (A7).
- "Biden warned Armageddon publicly" or "in a speech": it was a private fundraiser with a handful of reporters on 2022-10-06 (B6).
- "Petraeus said this in September 2022" or "US policy is to destroy Russian forces": it was 2022-10-02, a retired general's view, not US policy (B5).
- "Putin threatened nuclear strike on 2022-09-30 with 'Hiroshima'": he called the 1945 bombings a "precedent" but made no direct threat in that line; separate it from the 2022-09-21 "not a bluff" address (A29).
- "Plan A kills 34 million": say "about 91 million casualties, dead and injured" and give 34 million dead only with the Princeton study named; the NZ Herald piece itself gives both 31.4 and 34 million, and the ACA article says only "more than 90 million" dead and injured (A23). Plan A is a Russia-NATO full war, not a one-bomb simulation.
- "Russia drilled tactical nukes near Ukraine in October 2026": false. The Moscow Times page whose address contains 2026/10 is a 2024-05-21 article; the 2026 exercise was 2026-05-19 to 2026-05-21 (A5).
- "New START is still in force" or "Russia and the US have 1,550 limits": expired 2026-02-05 (A3).
- "Russia tested a nuclear weapon in 2025 or 2026": no explosive test occurred; Putin only ordered proposals on 2025-11-05 (A9).
- "Ukraine is building a nuclear weapon" or "Britain and France are arming Ukraine with nukes": Russian SVR claims without evidence, denied by Kyiv and London (A8).
- Any date, battlefield or political detail inside the simulation presented as real; any exact "when" for a real Russian nuclear decision.
