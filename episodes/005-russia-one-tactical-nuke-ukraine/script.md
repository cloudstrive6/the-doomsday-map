# Russia Uses One Tactical Nuke in Ukraine: Day by Day

Episode 005. Draft 1, 2026-11-10. About twelve minutes at 138 words per minute. Each paragraph is one narration unit; the tag on a section header is the on-screen corner tag. Refs in brackets are for the fact screener and are not read aloud.

## Cold open [SIMULATION]

Day one. Nine twenty in the morning. A missile leaves a launcher in Russia's Belgorod region, aimed at open farmland a hundred and thirty kilometres south. [C]

It looks like any other Iskander. Russia has fired them at Ukraine throughout this war. [A14, C]

In three minutes, that field is where the first nuclear weapon used in war since Nagasaki goes off. [B1, C]

This is a simulation built from published numbers, and it has not happened. [C]

The missile is already past the top of its arc. Nobody on the ground has been told what it carries. [C]

## Chapter 01: what Russia really has [REAL DATA]

Russia's forces hold about forty-four hundred nuclear warheads, by the Federation of American Scientists' count from March twenty twenty-six. About eighteen hundred of them are deployed strategic warheads: the long-range ones, on land, submarines and bombers. [A10, A11]

Separate from those are the ones this episode is about. Non-strategic, or tactical, warheads: somewhere between one and two thousand, according to US estimates. Aerial bombs, missile warheads, artillery shells. [A12, A13]

We found no published yield for them. We will come back to that. [A16]

What carries them matters more. The Iskander-M is a short-range ballistic missile with a range of four hundred to five hundred kilometres. It is the missile most often named as a tactical nuclear carrier. And Russia has fired Iskanders at Ukraine conventionally throughout this war. [A14]

One more fact sets the stage. In February twenty twenty-six, the last treaty limiting American and Russian arsenals expired. For the first time in decades, neither side has a cap. [A3]

## Chapter 02: how close it has come [REAL HISTORY]

Only two nuclear weapons have ever been used in war. Hiroshima, in August nineteen forty-five, about fifteen kilotons. Nagasaki, three days later, about twenty-one. None since. [B1, A22]

In October nineteen sixty-two, the Cuban Missile Crisis brought the United States and the Soviet Union to the brink. President Biden would use it, sixty years later, as the yardstick for the danger in Ukraine. [B2, B6]

In September twenty twenty-two, Vladimir Putin said Russia would use all the means at its disposal, and added: "It's not a bluff." Two weeks later, President Biden said the world had not faced the prospect of Armageddon since the Cuban Missile Crisis. He added that he did not think there was any such thing as easily using a tactical nuclear weapon. [A29, B4, B6]

The White House said at the time that it saw no sign of imminent use. [A1, B6]

Behind the scenes, the channels opened. American and Russian defence chiefs spoke by phone in October. In November, news reports said senior Russian officials had discussed tactical use, with no evidence that any warhead had been moved. Days later the head of the CIA met Russia's foreign intelligence chief in Ankara to warn against it. [B7, B8]

In November twenty twenty-four, Russia rewrote its doctrine. Nuclear use may be considered after a conventional attack that creates a critical threat to the sovereignty or territorial integrity of Russia or Belarus. It names no target and no moment. [A17]

And the signals have kept coming. In twenty twenty-six, Russia held a three-day nuclear exercise with Belarus. Its defence ministry said tactical nuclear weapons were part of the drills. In February, Dmitry Medvedev said Russia would use non-strategic nuclear weapons if Britain and France gave Ukraine nuclear technology. Ukraine and Britain deny any such plan. Russia has fired Oreshnik, a nuclear-capable missile, at Ukrainian territory, without a nuclear warhead. [A5, A8, A7]

Some commentators call the idea behind one-weapon use escalate to de-escalate. One small strike, meant to make the other side stop. It is an idea they describe. It is not a published Russian plan. [C]

How fast would it happen? An Iskander covers a hundred and thirty kilometres in about three minutes. [C]

## Chapter 03: the trigger [SIMULATION]

Here are the assumptions. Every one is ours, and none is a prediction. [C]

Before day one, a Ukrainian counter-offensive threatens a Russian-held sector. Moscow describes the loss as the kind of critical threat to territorial integrity that its doctrine mentions. That doctrine does not say what Russia would do. We are choosing. [A17, C]

We choose one weapon. One Iskander-M, one warhead, one target. [C]

The target is an invented staging area for Ukrainian reserves, in open farmland in eastern Kharkiv region. No city. Not near the Zaporizhzhia nuclear plant. The location is simulated, and no unit or person in it is real. [A27, C]

And the yield. We found no published yield for Russian tactical warheads. So we assume fifteen kilotons, the same as Hiroshima. [A16, A22, C]

## Chapter 04: minute by minute [SIMULATION]

Minute zero. The launch. Radar sees a ballistic missile climbing out of Belgorod region. It looks like every other Iskander. We assume nobody can tell the difference in flight. [A30, C]

Minute one. Air defence crews have seconds, not minutes. A three-minute flight leaves no meaningful warning. [C]

Minute two. It begins to fall. Over the field, a staging area of vehicles and tents sits under a cold winter sky. [C]

Minute three. The warhead detonates in the air above the field. Not on the ground. [C]

There is a flash brighter than the sun. Then a fireball. Then a shock wave that crosses the field faster than a sound can reach anyone to warn them. [C]

Minute four. The cloud climbs. Satellites in orbit register the flash within seconds. Radiation monitors in neighbouring countries begin to log numbers. [C]

By minute ten, two governments have already said two different things. Ukraine's air force reports a nuclear detonation. Russia's defence ministry says nothing, or says it was something else. [C]

## Chapter 05: one warhead, ring by ring [SIMULATION]

Here is what fifteen kilotons does to open farmland, from the scenario engine. [C]

The fireball: a hundred and eighty metres in radius. About three hundred and sixty metres across. Everything inside it is gone. [C]

Three hundred and forty metres out, the blast reaches twenty pounds per square inch. Almost nothing built survives that. [C]

At one point six seven kilometres, five pounds per square inch. Most ordinary buildings collapse or are badly damaged. That ring covers about eight point eight square kilometres. [C]

The flash burns farther than that. Third-degree burns reach one point nine kilometres, past the edge of the five-psi ring. [C]

At four and a half kilometres, one pound per square inch. Windows shatter, and flying glass becomes the hazard. [C]

Fallout. Because the burst is in the air, the fireball never touches the ground, and the engine reports little local fallout. We chose a wind from the west, so the thin cloud drifts east, toward the Russian-held side, away from Kharkiv. We give no dose and no contour. The engine supplies none. [C]

And we give no death toll. No published study covers this strike. [C]

That is one small weapon. Eight point eight square kilometres of farmland, and a world that has to decide what it means. [C]

## Chapter 06: the day after [SIMULATION]

Day one, the first hours. We assume the UN Security Council meets in emergency session, and so do the G7 and NATO. In twenty twenty-two, Washington said it had told Moscow privately of "decisive" consequences if it used a nuclear weapon in Ukraine. Now someone must decide what that means. [A21, C]

Ukraine is not a NATO member. A strike on its soil would not trigger Article 5, the alliance's mutual defence clause. [A21]

The twenty twenty-two channels reopen: the phone calls between defence chiefs, the meeting of intelligence heads. We assume they do, as they did before. [B7, B8, C]

Radiation agencies in neighbouring countries report what they see. For an air burst like this one, our modelling expectation is readings at background outside the immediate area. That is an expectation, not a measurement. [C]

Day two to three. We assume no ally answers with a nuclear weapon. A conventional response against Russian forces is one option; one report said a retired American general suggested as much in twenty twenty-two, and he was not speaking for his government. Sanctions and diplomatic isolation are announced. China and India distance themselves. Those are our choices. [A21, B5, C]

Russia says it will not use another weapon, for now. [C]

Day four to seven. Oil prices, grain prices and shipping insurance jump. Flights are rerouted away from the Ukrainian border. Poland and the Baltic states raise their alert levels. Rail through the target district stops. Banking links with Russia are cut. All of it is simulated. [C]

In this simulation the strike causes no blackout. A fifteen-kiloton air burst this low has no wide-area electromagnetic pulse. But fear spreads. Ukraine has the largest nuclear power plant in Europe, held by Russian forces. This year the IAEA's director called its off-site power extremely fragile. In this simulation, no one is sure what the next strike would hit. [A27, C]

Day seven. The war goes on. There is no ceasefire. No one knows whether a second weapon exists, or where. [C]

For eighty-one years, one rule held: after Nagasaki, nobody used one. On day seven, nobody can say if it can be mended. [B1, C]
