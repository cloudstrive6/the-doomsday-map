# One 10-Kiloton Nuke at Sea, and a US Carrier Strike Group (Simulation)

Episode 017. Revision 1, 2026-11-24. A single simulated burst over open water beside a generic carrier group, with real history and data first. No real ship, country or date is named. Each paragraph is one narration unit; source refs in brackets are for the fact screener and are not read aloud.

## Cold open [SIMULATION]

Half past midnight, open Pacific: a nuclear weapon bursts beside a US carrier group. [C]

This is a simulation built from published numbers, and it has not happened. [C]

Twelve minutes earlier. A radar operator marks a contact low over the water, too small for an airliner, too fast for a ship. Nobody has fired on this group in years. The deck crew are loading jets for a routine night launch, and the watch is quiet. [C]

Twelve minutes is all the warning there is. And the question the rest of this video answers is the one the crew would face in the first minute: what does a carrier group do when nobody knows who fired? [C]

## Chapter 01: a city on the water [REAL DATA]

Picture a flight deck at night. Jets launch off the bow, and a crew in colored shirts works under red lights, in wind that never stops. [A1, A2]

The ship is more than a thousand feet long and weighs about one hundred thousand tons. Two nuclear reactors drive it. About five thousand people live aboard, a small town with its own airfield. [A1, A2]

It never sails alone, and at night, with the lights low, the whole group looks like a scatter of dim stars. A strike group is the carrier with its air wing of sixty or more aircraft, usually three to five escorts, a supply ship and sometimes a submarine, spread over many miles of sea. [A3, A4]

Each of those ships is a separate target, and each sits at its own distance from any one point on the water. In our simulation the ships sit about two and a half kilometers apart, and that spacing is the whole point of the next few minutes. [A4, C]

Which raises an old question: what happens to a fleet that is hit by an atomic bomb? The answer is not a guess. The Navy tested it. [A22]

## Chapter 02: the test, the brink and the pullback [REAL HISTORY]

In nineteen forty-six, at Bikini Atoll, the US anchored about ninety surplus and captured ships in a lagoon and detonated two bombs of about twenty-three kilotons. The first burst in the air. It missed its aim point by well over half a kilometer, and five ships still sank. Most could be boarded within a day. [A22, A23, B1]

The second burst was underwater, ninety feet down. A column of water rose more than a mile. Eight ships sank or capsized, including a carrier that took about seven and a half hours to sink, and every ship that stayed afloat was soaked in radioactivity. Hosing helped once, then stopped working. The third test was cancelled because the Navy could not clean its ships. [A24, A25, A27, B2]

That is why our simulation uses an air burst. Baker was shallow, underwater and more than twice our size. [A24, C]

In nineteen sixty-two, a Soviet submarine, out of air and chased by a carrier group near Cuba, carried a nuclear torpedo of about ten kilotons. Launching it needed three officers to agree. One would not, and the boat surfaced. It came close. A weapon of that size, near a carrier group, is the picture this episode draws. [B5, C]

Thirteen years later the danger came from the ships themselves. In nineteen seventy-five a cruiser and a carrier collided off Sicily, and one nuclear-weapons analyst reports that the fire on the cruiser stopped a few meters from its nuclear magazine, and that both ships carried nuclear weapons. [B6]

Even in nineteen ninety there were more than five thousand American nuclear weapons at sea. Then in nineteen ninety-one the US ordered its tactical weapons off its ships, and the last were gone by nineteen ninety-two. [A7, A8, B7]

So a carrier group today has no tactical nuclear weapons aboard its ships, and the last treaty limits on the big arsenals ended in February twenty twenty-six. So how could a nuclear weapon reach one, in a simulation? [A7, A31, C]

## Chapter 03: the trigger [SIMULATION]

In our simulation, an unnamed attacker, with no flag and no country, fires one nuclear cruise missile from a submarine about two hundred and eighty kilometers away. The weapon is fictional. No published source gives a yield for any modern anti-ship missile, so we choose roughly ten kilotons, about the size of one Cold War nuclear rocket test. [A30, B4, C]

Why one? Because this episode is about one weapon, not a war. The group is a generic hexagon in open ocean: a carrier at the center, an escort at each corner, a supply ship inside. A typical group has three to five escorts. We draw six, for symmetry. [A4, C]

Real groups vary, and so does everything about a real night at sea. Ship icons on our map are drawn larger than life, because a destroyer at true scale would be a dot. [C]

The missile flies for twenty minutes. Whether the group sees it depends on one radar operator. Our simulation says they see it, and cannot be sure what it is. A missile low over the water gives a crew about as much time as a long phone call. [C]

## Chapter 04: minute by minute [SIMULATION]

Minute zero. The missile leaves the water. A submarine's launch is never seen from the surface, and the group is about two hundred and eighty kilometers away, beyond any lookout's eye. [C]

Minute eight. A contact appears on the screens of the escort nearest the attacker, low and fast. The watch officer calls it up the chain, and the chain asks for a second radar to confirm. [C]

Minute twelve. General quarters. Hatches close, loose gear is lashed down, and helicopters lift off to look. Nobody has an order to shoot yet, because nobody can say what the contact is. [C]

Minute seventeen. The escort turns toward the contact and its guns track it. The carrier turns into the wind, ready to launch, and every ship broadcasts the same two words on every channel: unidentified contact. [C]

Minute twenty. The missile reaches the escort nearest the attacker, a little east of the carrier. It does not hit the ship. It bursts over it, and for a moment the sea is brighter than day. [C]

## Chapter 05: one warhead, ring by ring [SIMULATION]

The fireball is about three hundred meters across. The escort beneath it is inside, and in our simulation it is destroyed. That is an assumption, not an engine result. [C]

Heavy blast, twenty pounds per square inch, reaches about three hundred meters. [C]

The moderate blast ring, five pounds per square inch, reaches one point four six kilometers and covers about six point seven square kilometers of sea. Third-degree burns on bare skin reach one point six two kilometers. [C]

The light blast ring, one pound per square inch, reaches three point nine five kilometers. [C]

Now the ships. The carrier and two escorts sit two and a half kilometers from the burst, outside the blast and burn rings and inside the light one. The supply ship is three and a half kilometers out. The three escorts on the far side are four to five kilometers away, outside every ring. [C]

Compare that with Bikini, where the first bomb missed its aim point by more than half a kilometer. Here a miss of one kilometer would move the whole picture, and no crew can count on the weapon landing where it is aimed. [A23, C]

The model draws rings on a flat map. It has no hull, no waves and no armor in it, and no open source says how a hardened modern warship answers a blast like this. So we do not say the carrier survives, and we do not say it sinks. The ship has a seawater wash-down that sprays the deck and hull, built for fallout and fire, and the crew would turn it on. [A5, C]

The engine finds little local fallout from an air burst. History agrees in one narrow way: after the first Bikini shot, most ships could be reboarded within a day. [A23, C]

## Chapter 06: the day after [SIMULATION]

The question was what a carrier group does when nobody knows who fired. In the first minute, the answer on the map is a ring of ships scattering. Escorts turn outward, at speed, so a second weapon could not catch them all. This is doctrine in our simulation, not an engine result. [C]

On the destroyed escort's position, a wide patch of dark water. Rescue boats and helicopters from the other ships head in, and a neighbor ship turns to look for survivors. Whether anyone is there to find is something we do not compute, and there is no death toll in this episode. [C]

The air burst leaves little local fallout, so there is no plume to flee on the map, at most a faint band drifting east. Teams with meters sweep the nearest hulls, and the wash-down runs. At Bikini, hosing helped once and then failed, so the crews would not count on it. [A5, A23, A27, C]

The sky above the group is the next change. Aircraft that launched before the burst have a deck to come home to only if it is still usable. In our simulation, a damaged deck closes landings for some of them, and the supply ship and the aircraft tankers matter more. [C]

Radios are the last. In our simulation, radar and radio links near the burst fail for a while, and the group has to speak by flag and light. Electromagnetic pulse is not modeled, and we do not claim it. [C]

At the end of the hour, the group is spread wide across the sea. On the carrier's bridge an officer watches the radar, and a message goes to shore: one burst, one escort gone, source unknown. The bridge holds the choice the whole episode led to: answer at once with nothing known, or wait and let the sea stay quiet. Nobody has decided. [C]
